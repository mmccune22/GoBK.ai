## Above median is the beginning of the calculation

For most people with primarily consumer debt, the means test starts with average income over the six full calendar months before filing. That figure is compared with the median income for your state and household size.

This is a specific legal calculation, not simply your annual salary or what arrived in your bank account last month. Some income, including Social Security benefits, is excluded.

If the calculated income is above the median, the next step is to subtract the deductions allowed under the means-test rules.

## Which expenses count?

The means test uses a combination of standardized allowances and qualifying actual expenses.

Some living expenses use IRS standards that depend on factors such as household size and location. Other deductions may include qualifying taxes, insurance, childcare, court-ordered support, necessary healthcare expenses, and payments on debts secured by property.

Mortgage and vehicle payments can make a significant difference, but the calculation follows specific rules. It is not as simple as subtracting every bill you currently pay from your income.

See [What is the bankruptcy means test?](/library/what-is-the-means-test)

## Why do some above-median households qualify?

A household can earn a substantial income and still have little left after the expenses the law recognizes.

For example, a family with qualifying mortgage payments, vehicle loans, childcare costs, and health insurance premiums may have a very different result from another household earning the same amount with fewer allowable deductions.

The outcome depends on the complete calculation. Your salary alone cannot tell you whether you qualify.

It also matters which version of the income figures and expense standards applies when you file. Online estimates can be useful for orientation, but they cannot reliably resolve every deduction or legal issue in an individual case.

## What if my circumstances recently changed?

The six-month average may not reflect what your finances look like today. A job loss, reduced hours, or a change in necessary expenses can make timing important.

The law also allows certain special circumstances to justify adjustments when there is no reasonable alternative. A serious medical condition or a call to active military duty are examples identified in the Bankruptcy Code.

These adjustments require documentation and an explanation. A recent income drop or unusually high expense does not automatically qualify; the circumstances and applicable court decisions matter.

## Is the means test the only requirement?

No. Passing the means test does not resolve every eligibility rule or possible objection to a Chapter 7 case. Your overall financial circumstances and the accuracy of your disclosures still matter.

Property protection is also a separate question. Qualifying for Chapter 7 does not necessarily mean everything you own is protected.

See [What are exemptions?](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions)

## What if Chapter 7 is not available?

Chapter 13 may provide relief through a repayment plan, generally lasting five years for above-median-income filers.

That does not necessarily mean paying every debt in full. The required payment depends on several factors, including projected disposable income, property value, and debts that must receive particular treatment.

Chapter 13 can also offer tools Chapter 7 does not, such as catching up on missed mortgage payments over time. But the plan must be affordable and meet the legal requirements.
