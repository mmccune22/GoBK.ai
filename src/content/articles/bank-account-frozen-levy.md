## First, find out what happened

Contact the bank and ask:

- Why is the account restricted?
- Who requested the restriction, and under what authority?
- How much money is affected?
- Is the money still being held, or has it been transferred?
- What deadline applies before the bank sends it elsewhere?
- How can you obtain the order, notice, and contact information for the department handling it?

Not every freeze is a creditor levy. Restrictions can also involve suspected fraud, identity verification, a dispute over ownership, or the bank’s own claim against you. Those situations require different responses.

For a collection-related restriction, identify whether it involves an ordinary judgment creditor, a tax authority, support enforcement, or another government agency. Some agencies can levy without first obtaining the kind of court judgment required for an ordinary credit card debt.

Keep copies of notices and account statements. Do not assume there is a standard waiting period that applies to every freeze.

## Money that may already be protected

### Direct-deposited federal benefits

Federal rules require banks to review accounts for certain electronically deposited benefits when processing covered garnishment orders.

The automatically protected amount generally is the lesser of:

- The qualifying benefit deposits during the two-month lookback period.
- The account balance when the review occurs.

For example, if qualifying deposits total $3,000 but the balance is $1,000, the automatically protected amount generally is $1,000—not $3,000.

Covered benefits include [Social Security](/library/file-bankruptcy-unemployed-retired), SSI, VA benefits, and certain federal retirement benefits. Special rules apply to federal-government and state child-support orders; IRS tax levies are outside this automatic-protection rule.

If protected funds appear to have been frozen, ask the bank to review how it calculated the protected amount.

### Additional exemptions

Money beyond the automatically protected amount may still qualify for protection under federal or state [exemption](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions) law. Older benefit deposits, wages, and other funds can require an exemption claim and supporting records.

State rules vary on whether deposited wages remain protected, how long protection lasts, and whether a separate bank-account exemption applies. Do not assume paycheck-garnishment limits automatically protect the same percentage after wages reach your account.

Read the levy papers for objection or exemption procedures. A court filing, agency request, or hearing may be necessary, and deadlines can be short.

## If the IRS levied the account

An IRS bank levy generally includes a 21-day holding period before the bank sends the affected funds to the IRS. That creates an opportunity to address errors, ownership questions, or grounds for release.

Contact the IRS promptly using verified information from the levy notice. Explain if the restriction prevents payment of basic living expenses and be prepared to provide financial records.

A release may be available based on hardship or another statutory ground, but releasing a levy does not eliminate the tax debt. State tax levies follow their own procedures; do not assume the federal 21-day rule applies.

## Where bankruptcy fits

Bankruptcy generally stops many collection actions when the [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay) applies. That may prevent further enforcement and provide a process for addressing the debt and affected funds.

But **money frozen before filing is not automatically released simply because the bank has not sent it to the creditor**.

State law can affect when ownership rights transferred and whether the creditor acquired a lien. The bank’s reason for holding the funds, the applicable exemptions, and turnover procedures also matter. Additional court action may be required.

The account balance must also be disclosed in bankruptcy. Protection from a creditor’s levy and protection from administration by a bankruptcy [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) are related but separate questions.

### Can money already taken be recovered?

Sometimes. Certain prefiling seizures may be recoverable as preferential transfers, commonly involving a 90-day review period. The same rules apply to [garnished wages](/library/can-bankruptcy-stop-wage-garnishment).

Recovery depends on the statutory requirements, defenses, ownership, and applicable exemptions. A particular dollar amount or a recent transfer does not guarantee a refund.

Recovered money may belong to the bankruptcy estate. In qualifying circumstances, the debtor may have rights to recover and exempt it.

## Protect access to essential funds lawfully

Identify upcoming rent, food, medication, and utility needs, along with scheduled automatic withdrawals. Ask which funds remain accessible and what process is available for obtaining release of protected money.

Keeping benefit deposits identifiable can make tracing easier. A separate account may help with recordkeeping, but mixing funds does not automatically erase every protection.

Do not transfer money to relatives, conceal an account, or move funds in violation of a court order. Opening another account does not erase the judgment or guarantee that future deposits are protected.

If the bank itself claims you owe it money, setoff rights and account agreements need separate attention. Changing banking arrangements should be part of a lawful plan, with complete records and disclosure.

## The bottom line

A frozen account requires prompt fact-finding: why it was restricted, where the money is now, and which deadlines and protections apply.

Exemption claims, agency relief, or bankruptcy may help. None guarantees an immediate release or recovery of every dollar.

Address both the immediate loss of access and the underlying debt so the same problem does not simply recur.
