## Your balance on the filing date

Money in your accounts on the day you file becomes part of the bankruptcy estate and must be listed. Whether you keep it depends on [exemptions](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions). Some states protect certain wages or benefits after deposit, some provide a wildcard amount that can cover cash, and some offer little room.

When the available exemption is small, timing can help. Filing after rent and regular bills are paid, when the balance is naturally lower, is legitimate. Moving money to relatives, withdrawing cash to keep it off the schedules, or understating a balance is not. Trustees review statements, and balances are easy to verify.

Checks you've written that haven't cleared are still in your account on the filing date. Tell your attorney about them, especially around rent or other large payments.

## Owing money to the bank that holds your account

If you owe a credit card, loan, or overdraft to the same bank or credit union that holds your deposits, it may have a right of **setoff**: applying your deposits to the debt you owe it. Credit union agreements in particular may cross-secure accounts and loans.

**Before you file,** a bank may be able to take funds this way. **After you file,** the automatic stay generally prevents setoff without court permission, but the bank may place a temporary hold on the disputed amount while it asks the court.

A common precaution is to open an account at an institution you owe nothing to and move your direct deposit there before filing. Ask your attorney whether that makes sense in your situation.

## Temporary holds

Some banks place a temporary hold on an account after learning of a bankruptcy, even when no debt is owed, to protect funds the trustee may claim. These holds are usually released once the trustee or your attorney responds, but they can be disruptive in the first days of a case. Local attorneys often know which institutions do this.

## Joint accounts and other people's money

An account with your name on it generally must be listed, even if the money really belongs to a parent, child, or partner. That can usually be explained, but it invites questions, so tell your attorney whose money is in the account and keep records.

Don't add or remove names on accounts shortly before filing without advice. Changes can look like transfers, which trustees review. See [What should I avoid before filing bankruptcy?](/library/what-to-avoid-before-filing)

## After you file

You can generally keep using your accounts for everyday expenses.

In **Chapter 7**, money you earn after filing generally is not part of the case. In **Chapter 13**, income earned during the case is part of the estate and supports your plan, but you still use your accounts normally and make plan payments.

If an account was frozen or levied before you filed, see [My bank account was frozen or levied](/library/bank-account-frozen-levy).
