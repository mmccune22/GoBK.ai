Here are some common myths, with links to the fuller answers.

## “They’ll take everything I own.”

Most people who file Chapter 7 keep their belongings. Exemptions protect certain property or a certain amount of its value, and most individual Chapter 7 cases involve no property being sold.

That does not mean everything is protected in every case. The type of property, its value, and the exemptions available to you matter. Chapter 13 may offer a way to keep property that would be at risk in Chapter 7.

See [What are exemptions?](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions)

## “I’ll lose my 401(k), IRA, or pension.”

Many retirement accounts have strong bankruptcy protections. Qualified employer plans and many IRAs can generally be protected, although the rules depend on the account. Inherited accounts and money withdrawn from retirement accounts can receive different treatment.

Cashing out protected retirement savings to pay debts that bankruptcy could eliminate may leave you worse off. Understand the protection before using that money.

See [What happens to my 401(k), IRA, or pension?](/library/retirement-accounts-in-bankruptcy)

## “Chapter 13 means paying back everything.”

Some Chapter 13 plans repay unsecured creditors in full. Others pay only a portion of those debts, with the remaining qualifying balances discharged after successful completion.

The required payment depends on more than your monthly budget. Your income, allowable expenses, property, and the kinds of debt you owe all matter.

See [How Chapter 13 works](/library/how-chapter-13-works).

## “My credit will be ruined for ten years.”

How long bankruptcy appears on a credit report is different from how long it affects your ability to rebuild.

Chapter 7 generally remains on credit reports for ten years, while Chapter 13 generally remains for seven. You can begin rebuilding before the bankruptcy disappears. Your progress depends on your starting point, accurate reporting, and how you manage your finances afterward.

There is no guaranteed recovery date or credit score. But a bankruptcy entry does not mean your credit is frozen in place for a decade.

See [How long does bankruptcy stay on my credit report?](/library/when-does-bankruptcy-fall-off-credit-report)

## “Everyone will know.”

Bankruptcy is a public court record. Someone who searches for your case can find it, and it can appear on your credit report. But friends, neighbors, and coworkers are not routinely notified simply because they know you.

Your creditors receive notice. Your employer may learn about the case in certain situations, such as stopping a wage garnishment or handling Chapter 13 payroll deductions. That is different from a general announcement to everyone in your life.

See [Will everyone know I filed? Is bankruptcy public?](/library/is-bankruptcy-public)

## “I make too much money to file.”

Income above your state’s median does not automatically disqualify you from Chapter 7. A further calculation considers allowable expenses and other factors.

Even if Chapter 7 is unavailable, Chapter 13 may provide relief. Income affects your options; it does not, by itself, settle the question.

See [What is the means test?](/library/what-is-the-means-test)

## “I already filed once, so I can’t file again.”

Bankruptcy is not a once-in-a-lifetime option.

Prior cases can affect when you qualify for another discharge, whether you can refile immediately, and how much protection the automatic stay provides. The outcome depends on the chapters involved, the filing dates, and how the earlier cases ended.

See [How often can you file bankruptcy?](/library/how-often-can-you-file)

## “I’ll have to stand in front of a judge and be interrogated.”

Most people must attend a meeting called the 341 meeting, or meeting of creditors. A trustee conducts it, and you answer questions under oath about your paperwork and finances. Creditors may also attend and ask questions.

It is not a trial, and no judge attends that meeting. Routine meetings are often brief, although additional questions or a continued meeting may be necessary. Some cases also require separate court hearings.

See [What is a 341 meeting?](/library/what-is-a-341-meeting)

## “Bankruptcy lets me keep my house or car without paying for it.”

Bankruptcy can eliminate your personal obligation to pay a debt without eliminating the lender’s lien—the right to recover the property securing the loan.

Keeping a financed home or car generally requires continuing to pay for it. Chapter 13 can help catch up on missed mortgage payments, but you must also be able to afford the ongoing payments.

Bankruptcy offers real tools for protecting property. It does not make a financed house or car free.

See [How Chapter 13 saves a house: curing mortgage arrears](/library/chapter-13-stop-foreclosure-catch-up-mortgage).

## “Filing means I failed.”

Financial trouble is a situation, not a character verdict.

Illness, job loss, divorce, business problems, rising expenses, and decisions that did not work out can all contribute to debt. You do not have to prove that every choice you made was perfect to deserve a way forward.

Bankruptcy is a legal tool for addressing debts you cannot reasonably manage. The useful question is whether it can improve your situation and help you build a more sustainable financial life.

If one of these fears has been shaping your decisions, follow the link to its full article. Understanding what bankruptcy actually does—and what it does not—gives you a better foundation for deciding what comes next.
