## Many overpayments can be discharged

An overpayment occurs when an agency determines that you received more benefits than you were entitled to receive. It can result from an agency error, delayed processing, changed income, or a reporting problem.

Owing money to a government agency does not, by itself, make the debt nondischargeable. Many ordinary overpayment debts can be discharged in Chapter 7 or addressed through Chapter 13.

But different programs have different rules. Social Security, Supplemental Security Income, unemployment benefits, SNAP, and other assistance programs should not be treated as one interchangeable category. The underlying debt, any penalties, and the agency’s recovery rights require separate review.

In Chapter 13, discharge generally follows successful plan completion; the plan may require payment of some or all of an eligible overpayment claim.

## The fraud exception

An agency may challenge discharge if it claims benefits were obtained through fraud or qualifying false representations.

Examples can include intentionally misstating income or knowingly submitting false unemployment certifications. But an incorrect payment does not automatically establish fraud. The reporting requirements, information provided, and circumstances surrounding the payment matter.

For fraud-based discharge challenges, the agency generally must file an adversary proceeding and prove the applicable legal requirements. The usual deadline is 60 days after the first date set for the [meeting of creditors](/library/what-is-a-341-meeting), subject to extensions and exceptions.

Do not assume an agency will decline to challenge the debt because the balance is small. Likewise, a fraud allegation is not itself a final determination that the debt survives.

## “At fault” is not necessarily the same as fraud

Agencies use their own standards when deciding whether to waive recovery of an overpayment. Those standards can differ from the bankruptcy fraud standard.

For example, a finding that you should have recognized an incorrect benefit payment may affect a waiver request without necessarily establishing that you obtained money through fraud.

But an agency decision should not simply be ignored. Prior findings, hearing records, admissions, settlements, or court judgments may affect the bankruptcy analysis. Whether an earlier decision has binding effect depends on the proceeding and applicable law.

The useful distinction is that **an “at fault” letter does not automatically settle dischargeability**, but the underlying record still matters.

## Will deductions from future benefits stop?

Filing bankruptcy generally stops many collection actions when the [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay) applies. A discharge generally prevents collection of covered debts as personal liabilities.

However, reducing future benefits can involve different legal mechanisms:

- **Setoff** generally involves applying one obligation against a separate obligation.
- **Recoupment** generally involves adjusting amounts arising from the same transaction.

Whether a particular deduction is permissible depends on the program, the relationship between the payments, and applicable court decisions. Calling a deduction “recoupment” does not automatically make it lawful.

[SSA’s own instructions](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0202215185) generally require stopping recovery of prefiling overpayments during bankruptcy, while recognizing limited circumstances in which its legal counsel may authorize continued recoupment.

Bankruptcy therefore may stop deductions, but **filing does not guarantee that your next benefit payment will immediately return to its full amount**.

If withholding continues, keep the benefit notices and payment records so the specific recovery action can be evaluated.

## Appeals and waivers may offer another path

Bankruptcy is not the only way to address an overpayment.

An **appeal** challenges whether the agency’s decision or calculation is correct. A **waiver request** asks the agency not to recover an overpayment even if it occurred. Some programs also allow a reduced recovery rate.

For Social Security, waiver generally requires that you were without fault and that recovery would defeat the program’s purpose or meet another applicable waiver standard. Financial hardship alone does not necessarily establish every requirement.

Other programs have their own procedures and eligibility rules. Read the notice carefully and preserve appeal deadlines; do not assume a bankruptcy filing extends every agency deadline.

## Penalties and other proceedings need separate attention

An agency may claim both repayment of benefits and an additional penalty. Those amounts can receive different bankruptcy treatment.

Certain [government penalties](/library/court-fines-tickets-restitution) that survive Chapter 7 may be dischargeable after completing Chapter 13. Fraud-based obligations and criminal restitution present different issues, and bankruptcy does not stop a criminal prosecution merely because it concerns the same benefits.

Identify each part of the claim rather than assuming that discharge of the overpayment also resolves every penalty or proceeding.

## The bottom line

Benefit overpayments are not automatically beyond bankruptcy relief. Many can be discharged or addressed through a repayment plan.

The answer requires identifying the program, the reason for the overpayment, any fraud findings or allegations, and how the agency is collecting. Appeals and waivers may provide additional options.

The goal is to resolve both the claimed debt and its effect on the benefits you depend on—not simply obtain a discharge while leaving ongoing withholding unexplained.
