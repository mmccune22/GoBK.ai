## The business that is you

If you work for yourself as a consultant, tradesperson, driver, or other service provider, Chapter 7 does not automatically prevent you from continuing to earn a living.

But your ability to work and your existing business property are separate questions. Tools, vehicles, cash, accounts receivable, and other assets must be disclosed and evaluated. Tools-of-the-trade [exemptions](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions) may protect qualifying equipment, but the available protection depends on applicable law, ownership, and value. Do not assume everything used for work is exempt.

**When the money was earned matters.** Earnings from services you personally perform after filing generally are not property of your Chapter 7 estate. Money owed for work performed before filing may belong to the estate even if the customer pays afterward. Income attributable to existing business assets can require additional analysis.

For example, an unpaid invoice for a job completed before filing is different from payment for a new job you perform after filing. Work spanning the filing date may require an allocation.

A service business with limited assets may be able to continue, but ongoing operations still need to be evaluated for estate-property issues, insurance, licensing, and potential liability.

## The business that owns things

A business with inventory, substantial equipment, receivables, or transferable goodwill presents different questions from one that depends almost entirely on your future labor.

If you are a sole proprietor, there is no separate legal entity between you and the business. Your business assets are evaluated in your personal bankruptcy, along with your other property. Exemptions, liens, ownership, and sale costs affect whether value is available for creditors.

The [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) may collect receivables, sell nonexempt assets, or take steps to stop operations that put estate property at risk. You cannot assume you remain free to sell inventory, spend business cash, or transfer equipment after filing.

Chapter 7 is a liquidation process, rather than a plan for keeping an asset-heavy business operating indefinitely. The court can authorize the trustee to operate a business temporarily when doing so benefits the estate and is consistent with an orderly liquidation.

If the business is viable and keeping its operating assets is essential, another chapter may fit better. Eligible self-employed individuals and sole proprietors can use [Chapter 13](/library/chapter-13-self-employed). Chapter 11, including its small-business provisions where applicable, may offer other reorganization options. These alternatives have their own eligibility, payment, and operating requirements.

## The LLC or corporation distinction

An LLC or corporation is legally separate from its owner. Your personal bankruptcy does not automatically place the company itself into bankruptcy.

Instead, your ownership interest—such as corporate shares or an LLC membership interest—generally becomes property of your personal bankruptcy estate, subject to applicable protections.

That distinction does not guarantee the business will remain unaffected. Depending on the entity, governing documents, applicable law, and ownership structure, the trustee may be able to sell your interest or exercise rights associated with it. With a solely owned company, those rights may allow the trustee to control the entity and pursue liquidation of its assets.

The value of an ownership interest is not automatically equal to the company’s gross assets or revenue. Business liabilities, liens, transfer restrictions, other owners’ rights, and dependence on your personal services can all affect value.

**Personal guarantees create a separate obligation.** If you guaranteed a business loan, lease, or other debt, the creditor may have a claim against you personally. Many ordinary contractual guarantees can be discharged, but exceptions apply. A government-backed business loan is not automatically nondischargeable merely because the government is involved.

Your personal discharge generally does not eliminate the company’s debt, release another guarantor, or remove valid liens on collateral.

### What if the company itself files Chapter 7?

An LLC or corporation can file its own Chapter 7 case. The trustee administers the company’s assets for creditors, and ordinary operations generally stop, subject to any authorized temporary operation.

An LLC or corporation does **not** receive a Chapter 7 discharge. A company filing also does not automatically discharge an owner’s personal guarantees. The company’s case and the owner’s personal obligations must be evaluated separately.

## Closing or reorganizing a struggling business

Closing before filing and filing while still operating can produce different results. The timing may affect receivables, leases, employee obligations, taxes, and what property the trustee must administer.

Closing the doors does not make business assets disappear. Neither does dissolving an entity automatically eliminate its debts or your personal liability.

Before making major changes, identify:

- Who owns the equipment, inventory, cash, and receivables.
- Which debts belong to you, the entity, or both.
- Which obligations are secured by property or personally guaranteed.
- Whether payroll, sales, or other taxes could create personal liability.
- Whether the business has enough ongoing value to justify reorganization.

Do not give away equipment, transfer the business to a relative, repay insiders, or move assets into a new company on the assumption that this will protect them from bankruptcy. Those transactions may be investigated or reversed and can create additional problems.

Certain business taxes, including qualifying trust-fund tax liabilities, may [survive a personal discharge](/library/can-old-tax-debt-be-discharged). Closing the business does not resolve those obligations by itself.

If preserving a viable business is the goal, Chapter 13 or Chapter 11 may deserve consideration before a Chapter 7 filing places essential assets or ownership rights under the trustee’s control.

## The bottom line

Chapter 7 does not automatically end your ability to work for yourself. But it can affect the property, receivables, and ownership interests connected to your business.

For a sole proprietor, business assets are part of the personal bankruptcy analysis. For an LLC or corporation, your ownership interest matters, and the trustee’s rights may affect control of the company. A separate company filing does not automatically resolve your personal guarantees.

The key is understanding what belongs to whom, which debts you personally owe, and whether your goal is to close the business or preserve it.
