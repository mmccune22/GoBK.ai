## Stopping the sale

Filing bankruptcy generally triggers the [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay), which prohibits a covered foreclosure sale from proceeding without court permission.

The petition must actually be filed. Hiring an attorney, gathering documents, or telling the lender you intend to file does not stop the sale.

Timing is critical. Applicable law determines when foreclosure has progressed too far for bankruptcy to preserve your ownership or your right to cure the mortgage default. Do not assume you have until the deed is recorded or eviction begins.

[Prior bankruptcies](/library/how-often-can-you-file) and certain orders affecting the property can limit or prevent stay protection. A sale conducted in violation of an applicable stay can be challenged, but the legal effect and available remedies depend on the circumstances and controlling law.

An emergency filing may be possible, but eligibility, required credit counseling, and deadlines for completing the paperwork still matter. See [How fast can I file if something is days away?](/library/how-fast-can-i-file)

## Keeping the house through Chapter 13

Chapter 13 can generally let you cure missed payments on a qualifying long-term mortgage through the plan while maintaining payments that come due after filing.

You do not need the lender to voluntarily approve that catch-up schedule if the court confirms a plan meeting the legal requirements. The lender can still object to inaccurate figures or treatment that does not comply with the law.

For a typical mortgage secured only by your principal residence, this generally means restoring the existing payment schedule—not forcing a lower principal balance or interest rate. Exceptions require separate analysis.

Your budget must cover:

- The plan payment, including mortgage arrears and other required obligations.
- The ongoing mortgage payment, whether paid directly or through the trustee.
- Taxes, insurance, maintenance, and other household expenses.

Do not count the ongoing mortgage twice if it is included in the trustee payment.

Nonexempt equity can also increase what the plan must pay unsecured creditors. A plan must meet the legal requirements and be financially feasible; stopping the sale alone does not establish either.

Certain wholly unsecured junior mortgages may qualify for lien removal in Chapter 13, but valuation, proper procedures, and completion requirements matter. Filing does not automatically eliminate a second mortgage.

See [How Chapter 13 saves a house: curing mortgage arrears](/library/chapter-13-stop-foreclosure-catch-up-mortgage) for the detailed explanation.

## What Chapter 7 can—and cannot—do

Chapter 7 may temporarily stop a foreclosure, but it does not provide a multi-year repayment plan for mortgage arrears.

The lender can request relief from the stay. Protection may also end at another statutory event, including discharge. There is no guaranteed number of weeks or months before foreclosure can resume.

Chapter 7 also requires evaluating your equity. A [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) may sell a house with sufficient nonexempt value (see [Will I lose my house if I file bankruptcy?](/library/will-i-lose-my-house-if-i-file-bankruptcy)), even if your original concern was the lender’s foreclosure.

If you hope to sell or negotiate during the case, account for the trustee’s rights and any necessary approval. Filing does not guarantee control over the sale or enough time to complete it.

## If you decide to let the house go

Bankruptcy may eliminate eligible personal liability on a mortgage or deficiency balance. Whether a lender could otherwise pursue a deficiency depends on state law, the loan, and the foreclosure process.

But discharge generally does not remove a surviving mortgage lien. It also does not transfer ownership back to the lender simply because you move out or state that you are surrendering the house.

That gap matters. While you remain an owner, obligations involving association assessments, insurance, taxes, or property conditions may continue.

In particular, postfiling HOA or condominium assessments can remain your personal responsibility in Chapter 7 while you retain a qualifying ownership interest. Chapter 13 treatment requires separate analysis. Do not assume filing before foreclosure ensures that every later property-related charge will be discharged.

## Alternatives to filing

Depending on your circumstances, a loan modification, repayment agreement, forbearance, sale, or [another arrangement](/library/alternatives) may resolve the default without bankruptcy.

Federal mortgage-servicing rules provide protections for certain timely, complete loss-mitigation applications. Timing and coverage matter: submitting an application shortly before a sale does not automatically postpone it.

For example, important federal review protections generally depend on the servicer receiving a complete application more than 37 days before the scheduled sale, subject to applicable rules and exceptions. Get confirmation of any postponement rather than relying on an application being “under review.”

A HUD-approved housing counselor can help explain mortgage-assistance options. Bankruptcy and loss mitigation may also be pursued together, with coordination of the applicable requirements.

## Things that change the answer

Key facts include:

- The exact sale date and current foreclosure stage.
- Prior bankruptcy cases or orders affecting the property.
- Mortgage arrears and ongoing payment requirements.
- Available income and realistic housing costs.
- Equity, ownership interests, and applicable exemptions.
- Other liens, taxes, and association obligations.

State procedures differ substantially. A timeline from another state—or another homeowner’s experience—may not describe the time available in your case.

## The bottom line

Bankruptcy can often stop a pending foreclosure, but preserving the house requires more than stopping the sale.

Chapter 13 may provide a workable way to catch up. Chapter 7 may offer temporary protection and relief from eligible personal liability, while creating separate equity considerations.

If a sale is approaching, establish the actual deadline promptly. Then evaluate whether the goal is keeping the house, arranging a sale, or leaving with a manageable financial outcome.
