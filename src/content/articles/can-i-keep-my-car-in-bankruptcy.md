## If you own the car outright

If there is no loan or other lien on the car, its current value generally represents your equity. A vehicle [exemption](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions)—and sometimes an available wildcard exemption—may protect that equity.

If the applicable exemptions fully protect your interest, a Chapter 7 [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) generally cannot sell it to pay ordinary unsecured creditors. You must disclose the car, provide a supportable value, and properly claim the exemptions.

If some equity is unprotected, a sale may be possible. The trustee considers whether the value available after exemptions and sale costs justifies administering the car. A modest amount of unprotected equity does not automatically mean a sale, but there is no universal safe amount.

In Chapter 13, you can often keep a car with nonexempt equity by funding a plan that meets the required minimum payments to unsecured creditors.

## If you are still paying on the car

Start with the equity calculation. A car worth $15,000 with a $12,000 loan payoff has approximately $3,000 in equity. Exemptions may protect that equity, but they generally do not eliminate the lender’s lien.

You therefore need to address two separate issues:

- Whether your equity is protected from the bankruptcy trustee.
- What is required to keep the car under the loan.

The options differ between Chapter 7 and Chapter 13.

## Your Chapter 7 options

### Reaffirm the loan

A reaffirmation agreement keeps you personally liable for a debt that otherwise could be discharged. You retain the car under the agreement and remain responsible for the payments.

That carries a significant risk: if you later default, the lender may repossess the car and pursue you for a remaining deficiency balance, subject to applicable law.

Reaffirmation has formal requirements and deadlines, and court approval may be necessary. Do not assume signing is a routine formality or that you must accept the lender’s proposed agreement.

### Redeem the car

For a qualifying vehicle securing a dischargeable consumer debt, redemption lets you pay the allowed secured claim in a lump sum. When the car is worth less than the loan balance, that can mean paying its court-determined value rather than the full debt.

The car must meet the statutory requirements, including being exempt or abandoned by the estate. The relevant value generally is replacement value considering age and condition—not simply a low trade-in offer.

Some people finance redemption, but the new loan’s interest, fees, and affordability matter. Replacing one expensive loan with another may offer little benefit.

### Continue paying without reaffirming, where available

Some borrowers retain a car by staying current and insured without entering an enforceable reaffirmation agreement. Whether that is available depends on applicable law, compliance with bankruptcy requirements, and the lender’s practices.

**Continuing payments alone does not guarantee protection from repossession in every Chapter 7 case.**

[The Chapter 7 article on reaffirmation, redemption, and keeping payments current](/library/reaffirmation-retain-redeem) explains these choices in more detail.

### Surrender the car

If keeping the car is unaffordable or no longer makes sense, you can surrender it.

Your personal liability for an eligible remaining loan balance generally is discharged in a successful Chapter 7 case, unless you have reaffirmed the debt or another exception applies. Surrender itself does not create the discharge, and your bankruptcy does not ordinarily release a co-borrower from their separate liability.

## Your Chapter 13 options

Chapter 13 can let you repay a car loan through a court-approved plan, potentially changing the interest rate and repayment schedule.

In qualifying cases, a **cramdown** divides the debt into a secured claim based on the car’s value and an unsecured claim for the remaining balance. The unsecured portion receives the treatment required by the plan, with any remaining eligible amount discharged after successful completion.

For a typical purchase-money loan on a vehicle acquired for personal use, that value reduction generally is unavailable if the debt was incurred within 910 days before filing. Even then, changing the interest rate or repayment schedule may help.

A lower payment is not guaranteed. Fitting a long-term loan into the plan’s repayment period can sometimes increase the monthly amount. You must also maintain required insurance and satisfy the plan’s other obligations.

See [Keeping your car in Chapter 13 (and what a cramdown is)](/library/chapter-13-keep-car-cramdown) for examples and additional details.

## If you lease the car

A lease gives you the right to use the vehicle under a contract; it does not give you ownership in the same way a financed purchase does.

Keeping the car generally requires maintaining payments and satisfying the requirements for continuing or assuming the lease. The process differs between Chapter 7 and Chapter 13, and existing defaults may need to be cured.

Returning the car and rejecting the lease may allow eligible lease-related claims to be discharged. But assuming a lease can create continuing obligations, so do not assume every charge will disappear if you return the car later.

A lease generally cannot be crammed down to the vehicle’s value like a qualifying purchase loan.

## If the car has already been repossessed

Chapter 13 may provide a way to recover a repossessed car if you still have rights in it, but filing does not automatically require its immediate return.

State law, whether the vehicle has been sold, adequate protection, and the proposed loan treatment all matter. Additional court action may be necessary.

If repossession is imminent or has just occurred, act promptly. See [Can bankruptcy stop a repossession, or get my car back?](/library/stop-repossession-get-car-back) in “Urgent problems” for the time-sensitive issues.

## Things that change the answer

The most important factors include:

- The car’s supportable current value and loan payoff.
- Which exemptions apply and how much equity they protect.
- Whether the loan is current.
- When the debt was incurred and what it financed.
- Whether the car is owned jointly or has a co-borrower.
- Whether payments, insurance, maintenance, and repairs fit your budget.

[Exemption amounts](/library/state-homestead-vehicle-exemptions) and eligibility rules vary. Recent moves can affect which laws apply, so your current state’s vehicle exemption is not necessarily the correct one for your case.

## The bottom line

Bankruptcy often provides a way to keep reliable transportation. The right approach depends on protecting your equity, addressing the loan or lease, and choosing payments you can maintain.

Chapter 7 may offer reaffirmation, redemption, or other available retention arrangements. Chapter 13 may provide additional tools to restructure the debt. If keeping the car would leave your budget unworkable, surrender may be a better path.

The goal is transportation you can afford after bankruptcy as well as during the case.
