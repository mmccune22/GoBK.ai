## The timing rules, in plain English

For ordinary income taxes, three timing rules provide a starting point:

- **The three-year rule.** The return’s due date, including applicable extensions, generally must be more than three years before the bankruptcy filing.
- **The two-year rule.** A late-filed return generally must have been filed more than two years before bankruptcy—but meeting this period does not necessarily make a late return eligible for discharge.
- **The 240-day rule.** The relevant assessment generally must fall outside the 240-day period before filing.

These are screening rules, not a guarantee. Certain taxes that remain assessable can still receive priority, and fraud or willful tax evasion can prevent discharge.

Assessment is the taxing authority’s formal recording of the liability. An audit or amended return may lead to an additional assessment with a different date; it does not necessarily restart the clock for every amount owed for that year.

[Prior bankruptcies](/library/how-often-can-you-file), offers in compromise, collection proceedings, and other events can suspend or extend relevant periods. Counting calendar days without checking those events can produce the wrong answer.

## Late and missing returns need special attention

**Filing an old return and waiting two years does not guarantee that the tax will become dischargeable.**

Courts differ on when a late-filed document qualifies as a “return” under bankruptcy law. Some apply strict rules that can prevent discharge even after the usual waiting periods have passed.

For example, the Tenth Circuit, which includes Colorado, has applied a restrictive interpretation to late-filed returns. This makes the return’s filing history particularly important—not just its age.

A substitute return prepared by the IRS under Internal Revenue Code § 6020(b) does not qualify as your filed return for this purpose. A different rule applies to certain cooperative returns prepared under § 6020(a).

Missing returns should be addressed, but do not assume that filing them creates a predictable countdown to discharge.

## Not every tax follows the income-tax rules

The familiar timing rules do not apply equally to every government obligation.

**Trust-fund taxes**, such as employee income taxes and the employee share of payroll taxes withheld from wages, generally cannot be discharged. Responsible individuals may face personal liability even when the business was a [separate entity](/library/business-in-chapter-7).

Other employment taxes, sales taxes, excise taxes, and property taxes require their own analysis. Calling everything “payroll taxes” or “business taxes” can hide important differences.

Taxes associated with fraudulent returns or willful evasion also face discharge restrictions.

## Penalties and interest have separate rules

Do not assume that the tax, penalties, and interest always receive identical treatment.

Interest associated with a dischargeable tax generally follows the underlying tax. Penalties have additional rules involving their purpose, the underlying obligation, and the event that triggered them.

In Chapter 7, some older penalties may be dischargeable even when the underlying tax survives. A completed Chapter 13 plan can provide broader relief from certain penalties.

Conversely, interest on a nondischargeable tax can sometimes continue accruing as your personal obligation during bankruptcy, even when it is not paid through the plan.

## Tax liens can survive discharge

Eliminating personal liability for a tax does not necessarily eliminate a valid tax lien.

A surviving lien may remain enforceable against property to which it attached before filing, including property protected by certain bankruptcy exemptions. You might discharge personal liability yet still need to resolve the lien when selling or refinancing a home.

Whether a lien exists, whether notice was properly filed, what property it reaches, and how it can be treated in bankruptcy are separate questions. Do not assume the absence of a lien on your credit report means none exists.

## How Chapter 13 can help

Chapter 13 may provide a structured way to address tax debt that cannot be eliminated immediately.

Priority tax claims generally must be paid in full through [the plan](/library/chapter-13-monthly-payment) unless the taxing authority agrees to different treatment. Older dischargeable unsecured taxes may receive the same treatment as other general unsecured debts. Secured tax claims require separate treatment based on the lien and applicable law.

Importantly, **nondischargeable does not always mean priority**. Some nonpriority taxes can survive the case even if the plan pays them only partially.

Bankruptcy generally stops many collection actions while the [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay) applies, but it does not halt every tax-related activity. Tax authorities may still conduct audits, assess taxes, issue certain notices, or take other actions permitted by law.

Chapter 13 also does not provide a blanket interest-and-penalty freeze. Its value depends on the classification of each tax claim and what will remain due after completion.

You must also comply with required return filings and [stay current on new tax obligations](/library/chapter-13-self-employed). Resolving old taxes while accumulating new ones can undermine the plan.

## Build the timeline before choosing a filing date

Tax account transcripts, filed returns, assessment records, extension information, and records of prior collection or bankruptcy proceedings help establish the timeline.

Transcripts are useful, but they may not answer every question by themselves. State taxes require records from the relevant state agency as well.

Timing can materially change the result. Waiting may help in one situation and expose you to collection or other risks in another. The decision requires a complete picture rather than a rule-of-thumb calculation.

## The bottom line

Bankruptcy can eliminate some tax debts and provide a repayment structure for others. The answer depends on the tax type, return history, assessment dates, applicable waiting periods, and any liens.

Evaluate each tax year and obligation separately. The goal is to understand both what the bankruptcy can resolve and what liability or lien may remain afterward.
