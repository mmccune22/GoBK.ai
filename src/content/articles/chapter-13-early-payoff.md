## Paying ahead is different from finishing early

A Chapter 13 plan involves both payment requirements and a repayment period. It is not necessarily a fixed loan balance that you can pay off whenever you choose.

For example, suppose your plan calls for $500 a month for 60 months. After 24 months, the remaining scheduled payments total $18,000. Sending the trustee $18,000 does not necessarily mean you have completed the plan or become entitled to a discharge.

When the applicable commitment period governs, it is generally three years for below-median households and five years for [above-median households](/library/above-median-income-now-what). The Bankruptcy Code permits a shorter period when the plan provides for payment in full of all allowed unsecured claims. How those requirements apply to an already-confirmed plan can depend on the plan and controlling court decisions.

Before sending extra money, determine whether it would shorten the case, cover future installments, or increase the amount creditors receive. Do not assume an advance payment gives you permission to stop making monthly payments.

## When early completion may be possible

Paying allowed unsecured claims in full, along with satisfying the plan’s other obligations and costs, can provide a path to early completion.

That amount may be much larger than the remaining scheduled payments in a plan that originally proposed paying unsecured creditors only a percentage of their claims.

You need a current payoff calculation that accounts for:

- Allowed claims and payments already distributed.
- Remaining secured and priority obligations covered by the plan.
- Trustee fees and unpaid approved attorney fees.
- Any interest or other amounts legally required.

Paying a plan in full does not necessarily mean paying off your entire mortgage. A [long-term mortgage](/library/chapter-13-stop-foreclosure-catch-up-mortgage) being maintained beyond the plan ordinarily continues afterward.

Even with sufficient funds, completion requires the applicable procedures and other discharge requirements. A lump-sum payment does not automatically produce a discharge.

## If your income increases

A raise, bonus, or new job does not automatically turn your plan into a full-payment plan. But an improvement in your finances can lead to a request to increase payments.

Before plan payments are completed, the debtor, trustee, or an allowed unsecured creditor may request a modification. Whether an increase is appropriate depends on the circumstances and the legal standards applied by your court.

A higher paycheck also may come with higher necessary expenses. The relevant question is how the change affects your financial circumstances and the plan’s requirements—not simply whether your gross income went up.

Follow your plan’s reporting requirements and tell your attorney about significant changes.

## If you receive an inheritance, settlement, or other lump sum

The source of the money matters. An inheritance, lawsuit settlement, gift from a relative, or proceeds from selling property can raise different questions about ownership, exemptions, disclosure, and plan modification.

Chapter 13 can reach property acquired after filing. In particular, do not assume that an inheritance is outside the case simply because it arrives more than 180 days after filing—the rule many people associate with Chapter 7 does not provide the same cutoff in Chapter 13.

Tell your attorney promptly when you learn you may be entitled to money, even if it has not arrived. Before spending, transferring, or using it to pay off the plan, determine:

- Whether it must be disclosed to the court or trustee.
- Whether any portion is protected by an [exemption](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions).
- Whether it affects the amount creditors must receive.
- Whether approval is needed for the proposed use.

An exemption may protect some funds, but it does not automatically resolve every question about disclosure or plan treatment. Likewise, money offered by a family member does not automatically allow early completion of a partial-payment plan.

## If the real goal is to leave Chapter 13

Sometimes “Can I pay it off early?” means “My circumstances have changed, and I want to understand my options.”

A modification may be possible. If repayment has become unworkable, conversion to Chapter 7 or a hardship discharge may deserve consideration. Those options have separate eligibility requirements and can change what happens to your property and debts.

Dismissal ends the case, but ordinarily provides no discharge and allows collection to resume. It should not be confused with successfully completing the plan.

These alternatives are discussed in [What happens if I can’t finish my Chapter 13 plan?](/library/what-if-i-cant-finish-chapter-13)

## The bottom line

Early completion is sometimes possible, particularly when the plan pays allowed unsecured claims in full and satisfies its other requirements. Paying the remaining scheduled installments ahead of time may not accomplish the same thing.

Before making a lump-sum payment, establish the actual payoff amount, how the money’s source affects the case, and what steps are required to finish. The goal is a completed plan and a discharge—not simply a larger payment sent sooner.
