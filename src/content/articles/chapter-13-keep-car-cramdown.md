## First, the emergency part

Filing Chapter 13 generally triggers the [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay), which stops a pending repossession. Exceptions apply, including restrictions arising from certain [prior bankruptcy filings](/library/how-often-can-you-file). The lender can also ask the court for permission to repossess if its interests are not adequately protected.

If the car has already been repossessed, getting it back is more complicated. Chapter 13 may provide a way to recover it, depending on your rights under state law, whether it has been sold, and the proposed treatment of the loan.

**Filing alone does not automatically require the lender to return a repossessed car.** Additional legal action may be necessary, and insurance and adequate protection for the lender can be important.

If repossession is imminent or has just happened, see [Can bankruptcy stop a repossession, or get my car back?](/library/stop-repossession-get-car-back) in “Urgent problems.”

## The cramdown, plainly

Suppose your car is worth $9,000, but you owe $16,000.

If the loan qualifies for a cramdown, the plan can divide that debt into two parts:

- **A $9,000 secured claim**, paid through the plan with the required interest.
- **A $7,000 unsecured claim**, treated with other general unsecured debts, such as credit cards and medical bills.

The unsecured portion receives whatever [payment the plan requires](/library/chapter-13-monthly-payment) for that class of creditors. That could be a small percentage, a larger percentage, or payment in full. Any remaining eligible balance can be discharged after successful completion of the plan.

The car’s value must be supportable. Bankruptcy generally uses replacement value for a personal-use vehicle, taking its age and condition into account—not simply the lowest trade-in offer you can find.

## What the 910-day rule means

For a typical purchase-money loan secured by a vehicle acquired for your personal use, you generally cannot reduce the secured balance to the car’s value if the debt was incurred within the 910 days before filing.

That means a qualifying recent car loan generally must be paid in full, with the required interest, if you keep the car through the plan over the lender’s objection.

If the debt was incurred more than 910 days before filing, a value-based reduction may be available. Different rules can apply to refinanced loans, business-use vehicles, and loans that were not used to purchase the car.

The relevant date is when the debt was incurred, not simply how long you have owned or driven the vehicle. Timing can materially affect the loan’s treatment, but delaying a filing also carries risks if repossession or another collection action is approaching.

## Interest and the repayment schedule

Even when the 910-day rule prevents a balance reduction, Chapter 13 may still change the interest rate and payment schedule.

For many car loans, the court-approved rate follows a formula that starts with the prime rate and adds an adjustment for risk. It is not automatically the contract rate, and it is not guaranteed to be a single-digit rate or lower than your existing rate.

A high-interest loan may become substantially less expensive. A loan that already has a low promotional rate may offer less opportunity for savings.

Repayment through the plan can also change the monthly amount. Spreading a balance over a longer period may lower the payment, while fitting a longer-term loan into a shorter plan may increase it. The result depends on the balance, interest rate, plan length, and other required payments.

Keeping the car also means maintaining required insurance and making the payments your case requires.

## Other options and complications

### Surrendering a car you cannot afford

You can propose to surrender the car rather than keep paying for it.

The lender may have a remaining claim after applying the vehicle’s sale proceeds. Any allowed unsecured deficiency is generally treated with your other unsecured debts and may be discharged after successful completion, unless an exception applies.

Surrender does not itself erase the loan, and it does not necessarily make the lender immediately transfer the title or collect the vehicle.

### A car that breaks down or is totaled during the plan

A major repair or accident can affect both your transportation and your ability to complete the plan.

Possible responses include using insurance proceeds, seeking approval for a replacement loan, or [modifying the plan](/library/what-if-i-cant-finish-chapter-13). But surrendering a vehicle after confirmation does not automatically turn the remaining secured debt into dischargeable unsecured debt. Courts differ on what changes are permitted, and the confirmed plan matters.

Before selling or trading the car, spending insurance proceeds, or signing a replacement loan, tell your attorney what happened so the necessary approvals and treatment can be addressed.

### Title loans

A loan secured by a car you already owned may receive different treatment from a loan used to buy the vehicle. The 910-day purchase-money restriction often does not apply.

Chapter 13 may allow a reduction of the secured claim to the vehicle’s value and a change in interest. But title-loan and pawn arrangements vary, and state law can affect whether you still own the vehicle when you file. A transaction labeled a “title loan” does not guarantee a particular result.

## The bottom line

Chapter 13 can help you keep a car by restructuring its loan, catching up through the plan, and sometimes reducing the secured balance to the vehicle’s value.

The available treatment depends on the loan’s purpose and age, the car’s value, and the legal requirements of your case. Keeping the vehicle must also fit your overall budget, including insurance, maintenance, and your other plan obligations.

The goal is reliable transportation you can afford throughout the plan—not simply a lower loan balance on paper.
