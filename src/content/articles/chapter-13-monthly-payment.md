## What the plan needs to cover

Start with the debts and costs your plan must address. Depending on your circumstances, these may include:

- **Missed mortgage payments.** If you are using Chapter 13 to [catch up on your mortgage](/library/chapter-13-stop-foreclosure-catch-up-mortgage), the plan generally spreads the arrears and allowable charges over the repayment period. You must also maintain ongoing mortgage payments.
- **Car loans and other secured debts.** The amount depends on which property you keep and how the plan treats each loan. Some qualifying loans can be [reduced to the collateral’s value](/library/chapter-13-keep-car-cramdown); others require payment of the full balance, with interest.
- **Priority debts.** Certain taxes and past-due child support or alimony generally must be paid in full, subject to limited exceptions. Not every tax debt receives the same treatment.
- **Attorney fees.** Fees approved for payment through the plan become part of what the plan must fund. How much is paid before filing varies.
- **Trustee fees.** The trustee collects a percentage of payments received through the plan. That fee must be included when calculating the required payment.

Adding required payments and costs, then spreading them over the plan’s term, provides a starting estimate. Interest, payment timing, and other requirements can affect the actual number.

Your trustee payment also may not include every obligation. Depending on the plan and local practice, you may pay your ongoing mortgage or other debts directly. Those payments still need room in your household budget.

## How income and expenses affect the payment

The next question is what your income and allowed expenses require you to pay toward unsecured debts, such as credit cards and medical bills.

This involves a legal calculation called **projected disposable income**. It is related to your household budget, but it is not always the same as the amount left in your checking account at the end of the month.

For below-median households, the calculation generally focuses on actual income and reasonably necessary expenses. For above-median households, the [means test](/library/what-is-the-means-test)’s expense rules also apply. Some deductions use standardized amounts; others depend on actual qualifying expenses.

The calculation can also account for known or virtually certain changes in income or expenses. A past paycheck does not always tell the whole story about what you can pay going forward.

These requirements must be considered together with the debts and fees the plan needs to cover. It is not always as simple as adding “everything left over” to a separate base payment.

## How your property affects the payment

Unsecured creditors generally must receive at least as much through Chapter 13 as they would receive if you filed Chapter 7.

That matters if you own property with equity that bankruptcy [exemptions](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions) do not protect. Equity generally means the property’s value after subtracting loans secured by it. The Chapter 7 comparison also considers exemptions, likely sale costs, and other amounts that would reduce what creditors actually receive.

This is one way Chapter 13 can help you keep property that might otherwise be sold. Instead of surrendering it to a Chapter 7 [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee), you fund a plan that meets the required minimum for unsecured creditors.

The property requirement and the disposable-income requirement overlap: you must satisfy both, but you generally do not add the two minimums together as separate payments to the same unsecured creditors.

## Why two households with the same debt pay differently

Imagine two households that each owe $30,000 in credit card debt.

One has modest income, no mortgage arrears, and no property that would produce money for unsecured creditors in Chapter 7. Its plan may pay only a small portion of those credit card balances.

The other has more disposable income, missed mortgage payments to cure, and property with nonexempt equity. Its required payment may be substantially higher.

The credit card balances are identical. The financial circumstances—and what the plans must accomplish—are different.

That is why a debt total alone cannot tell you your Chapter 13 payment. An online calculator may provide a rough illustration, but a useful estimate requires accurate information about your income, expenses, property, and debts.

## Why the proposed payment can change

The payment in your initial plan is a proposal. The trustee and creditors can review the supporting information and object if they believe the plan does not meet legal requirements.

Questions may involve income, claimed expenses, property values, or the amount owed on a particular debt. Resolving those questions can change the payment before the court approves the plan.

The goal is a payment that satisfies the law and is realistic enough to maintain. A budget should account for reasonably necessary expenses, including costs that do not arrive every month, such as vehicle maintenance or annual insurance bills.

If the required payment exceeds what your household can support, the answer may involve changing the plan’s length, reconsidering which property to keep, or evaluating another bankruptcy option. The court cannot approve an unworkable plan simply because the debts need attention.

## The bottom line

Your Chapter 13 payment comes from several requirements working together: the debts and fees the plan must cover, your projected disposable income, and the minimum unsecured creditors must receive based on your property.

Your total monthly obligations matter too. A trustee payment that looks manageable on its own may be unaffordable once you add a mortgage, support payments, and ordinary living expenses.

A useful payment estimate starts with your actual numbers. A successful plan needs both a legally sufficient payment and a realistic household budget.
