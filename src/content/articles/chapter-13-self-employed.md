## You do not need a traditional paycheck

“Regular income” does not mean you must receive a W-2 or earn exactly the same amount every month. Freelancers, contractors, gig workers, sole proprietors, and other business owners can qualify for Chapter 13.

The question is whether your income is sufficiently stable and regular to support the proposed payments. A seasonal business may qualify, but the plan must account for its actual income pattern rather than assume every month will look like the busiest one. You must also meet Chapter 13’s other eligibility requirements.

## Your records need to show what you actually earn

Gross business receipts are not the same as money available for household expenses and plan payments. The financial picture needs to show what comes in, the necessary costs of earning it, and what remains.

The case generally relies on records such as:

- Profit-and-loss statements showing business income and expenses.
- Business and personal bank statements.
- Filed tax returns.
- Records of invoices, customer payments, and significant expenses.
- Information about business assets, loans, and personal guarantees.

A separate business account and consistent bookkeeping make these numbers easier to explain. Avoid counting the same expense twice or treating personal spending as a business deduction.

You may also need operating reports or updated financial information during the case. The required format and frequency depend on your circumstances and local requirements.

Incomplete records do not necessarily mean you should delay filing for months to perfect the bookkeeping when a foreclosure, repossession, or other deadline is approaching.

## Build a plan around realistic cash flow

A business can be profitable over a year and still struggle to make a payment during a slow month.

Your budget should account for ordinary operating costs, seasonal changes, and reasonably predictable expenses such as insurance renewals, equipment maintenance, and taxes. It also needs to show how much money the business can reliably provide for your household.

The plan must satisfy legal payment requirements as well as fit your cash flow. Choosing a payment based only on your lowest-income month may not satisfy the law, while using your best month may create an unsustainable obligation.

Depending on local practice and the plan’s requirements, a structured payment schedule may help address predictable income changes. Whatever the arrangement, the court must find that you can make the proposed payments.

## Continuing to operate during Chapter 13

Chapter 13 generally allows a qualifying debtor engaged in business to continue operating, subject to court oversight and applicable restrictions.

That does not mean every transaction is permitted without approval. Ordinary business operations are different from taking out a new secured loan, selling significant assets, or making an unusual transfer. The court’s orders, local rules, and the type of transaction determine what approval is needed.

If you operate through an [LLC or corporation](/library/business-in-chapter-7), remember that **your Chapter 13 is your personal bankruptcy case**. The company does not become a Chapter 13 debtor simply because you own it. Your ownership interest and personal obligations must be disclosed, but company property and company debts require separate analysis.

## Stay current on taxes

A plan that addresses old debt can still become unworkable if new taxes accumulate.

Self-employed people often need to make estimated tax payments because taxes are not withheld from their business income. Build required federal and state payments into the budget rather than treating taxes as whatever is left at year-end.

If you have employees, payroll withholding and required tax deposits need particular attention. Certain unpaid trust-fund taxes can create personal liability and generally [are not discharged](/library/can-old-tax-debt-be-discharged).

Chapter 13 also requires filing applicable returns for tax periods ending during the four years before the bankruptcy filing. Those returns generally must be filed by the day before the first scheduled [meeting of creditors](/library/what-is-a-341-meeting), subject to limited extensions. Older unfiled returns may still require attention under tax law.

Continue filing required returns and paying current taxes during the case. Failure to do so can threaten the bankruptcy. Although the Code provides a mechanism for certain postfiling tax claims, you should not assume you can simply add new tax debt to the existing plan.

## What the plan can do for business-related debt

Chapter 13 may help reorganize business-related debts that you personally owe. Depending on the debt and collateral, the plan may:

- Restructure a loan secured by equipment or a vehicle.
- Catch up on qualifying secured obligations.
- Pay certain priority tax debts over time.
- Treat eligible supplier balances, business credit cards, and personal guarantees as unsecured claims.

The details matter. The 910-day restriction discussed in the [car-loan article](/library/chapter-13-keep-car-cramdown) concerns qualifying purchase-money loans for vehicles acquired for personal use. A work vehicle may receive different treatment, depending on its actual use and the loan. Other collateral has its own rules.

Likewise, back taxes do not all receive the same treatment. Priority status, tax liens, interest, penalties, and dischargeability must be considered separately.

Your discharge can eliminate eligible personal liability for a company debt you guaranteed. It does not discharge the separate company’s obligation or automatically prevent collection against company assets.

## The bottom line

Self-employment does not rule out Chapter 13. A viable business can provide the income needed to fund a plan while you address personal and business-related obligations.

The plan needs reliable records, realistic cash-flow projections, and room for ongoing taxes and operating expenses. It also needs a clear distinction between what you owe personally and what a separate business entity owes.

Chapter 13 can help reorganize debt, but the business must still generate enough income to support its operations, your household, and the required plan payments.
