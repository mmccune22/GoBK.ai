## What happens when you file

Filing Chapter 13 generally triggers the [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay), which stops a pending foreclosure. If the stay applies and the filing occurs before the legal cutoff, a scheduled foreclosure sale generally cannot proceed without permission from the bankruptcy court.

Timing matters. Filing after a foreclosure sale may be too late to use Chapter 13 to catch up on the mortgage and keep the house. The cutoff depends on applicable law, so do not assume you have until a deed is recorded or an eviction begins.

[Prior bankruptcy cases](/library/how-often-can-you-file) can also limit or prevent the automatic stay. Filing a new case does not always create the same protection as a first filing.

If a sale is approaching, treat the deadline as urgent. See [How fast can I file if something is days away?](/library/how-fast-can-i-file)

## How catching up works

The missed payments and other amounts needed to bring your mortgage current are called **mortgage arrears**.

Chapter 13 can let you pay those arrears over the plan, usually three to five years, while maintaining the mortgage payments that come due after filing. The arrears may include missed installments, escrow shortages, and fees or costs allowed by the loan agreement and applicable law.

This is called **curing the default**. Even if the lender has demanded the entire loan balance because you fell behind, Chapter 13 can generally let you restore the original payment schedule by curing the default and maintaining payments.

You do not need the lender to voluntarily approve a loan modification to use this process. The lender can challenge the proposed treatment, but the court can approve a plan that meets the legal requirements over the lender’s objection.

## Can your budget support keeping the house?

Catching up on the past is only part of the calculation. You also need to afford the mortgage going forward.

Your budget must cover:

- The ongoing mortgage payment, including required escrow amounts.
- The [Chapter 13 payment](/library/chapter-13-monthly-payment), which funds the mortgage arrears and other required plan obligations.
- Your remaining household expenses.

Depending on the plan and local practice, the ongoing mortgage payment may go directly to the servicer or through the trustee. If it goes through the trustee, it is already included in the trustee payment—do not count it twice.

Dividing the arrears by the number of plan months gives a rough starting point for the catch-up amount. The actual payment also needs to account for trustee fees, other plan costs, and any additional required debt payments.

If keeping the house is unaffordable, a Chapter 13 plan may instead provide time to sell it in an orderly way. That requires a workable proposal and compliance with court requirements; filing alone does not guarantee unlimited time or preserve a particular amount of equity.

## Details that matter during the case

### Review the lender’s claim

The mortgage company generally files a proof of claim identifying the amount owed and the arrears. Those figures should be checked against your records.

Incorrect payment histories, duplicate charges, or unsupported fees can affect what you are asked to pay. Your attorney can challenge amounts that are inaccurate or not legally recoverable.

If the lender fails to file a timely claim, the debtor or trustee may file one on its behalf within a separate deadline. A missing claim does not mean the mortgage or lien disappears.

### Watch payment changes and added fees

Your mortgage payment may change during the plan because of an escrow adjustment, an adjustable interest rate, or another permitted change.

Bankruptcy Rule 3002.1 requires notices of payment changes and certain fees for covered home-mortgage claims. It also provides procedures to determine the mortgage’s status during the case and whether the default has been cured and required payments made at the end.

These procedures help identify disputes. They do not replace reviewing statements, keeping payment records, and responding when something looks wrong.

### Stay current on new payments

Paying the old arrears through the trustee does not excuse missed mortgage payments after filing.

If you fall behind again, the lender may ask the court to lift the stay and allow foreclosure to resume. New missed payments can also complicate completion of the plan and, depending on the circumstances and applicable court decisions, your discharge.

Tell your attorney promptly if a payment is missed or your income changes. A modification or another solution may be available, but it must fit the remaining plan term and legal requirements.

## What Chapter 13 generally cannot change

For a typical long-term mortgage secured only by your principal residence, Chapter 13 generally cannot force the lender to reduce the principal balance, lower the interest rate, or replace the contractual payment with one you prefer.

The usual tool is to catch up on missed payments while maintaining the existing loan.

Exceptions can apply. Certain mortgages that mature before the plan ends, wholly unsecured junior mortgages, and loans secured by other property may receive different treatment. A voluntary loan modification may also be possible during the case, subject to applicable requirements.

Those exceptions require separate analysis. Filing Chapter 13 does not automatically reduce your mortgage payment or eliminate a second mortgage.

## The bottom line

Chapter 13 can turn a demand to catch up immediately into a repayment schedule spread over several years. That can make the difference between losing a house and keeping it.

The plan must address both the missed payments and the mortgage going forward. Its success depends on timely filing, available stay protection, accurate mortgage figures, and enough income to maintain the required payments.

If foreclosure is approaching, time matters. Chapter 13 works best when there is enough time to build a realistic plan before the legal deadline passes.
