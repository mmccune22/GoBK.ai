## The routine timeline

Several weeks after filing, you attend the [341 meeting](/library/what-is-a-341-meeting) of creditors. Certain objections to discharge or to the dischargeability of particular debts generally must be filed within 60 days after the **first date set for that meeting**.

That starting date matters. Postponing or continuing the meeting does not automatically restart the objection period. The court can extend certain deadlines when the applicable requirements are met.

Once the relevant deadlines expire, the court generally enters the discharge promptly if all requirements are satisfied and nothing prevents entry. In a straightforward case, expect roughly 60 to 90 days after the first scheduled meeting date, or about three to four months after filing. This is a typical range, not a guaranteed deadline.

The discharge order removes your personal liability for dischargeable debts and prohibits creditors from collecting those debts from you personally. It does not necessarily eliminate liens or debts that the law excludes from discharge.

Keep the order and your bankruptcy paperwork. The discharge order generally does not list which individual debts were discharged.

## Requirements and proceedings that can delay discharge

### Missing debtor education or proof of completion

Most individual filers must complete an approved [debtor education course](/library/credit-counseling-and-debtor-education) after filing. Completion must also be properly documented with the court.

In Chapter 7, the ordinary deadline is 60 days after the first date set for the creditors’ meeting, unless the court extends it. Some providers report completion directly; others give you a certificate that must be filed.

If this requirement is not satisfied, the case may close without a discharge. Correcting the problem later may require reopening the case, additional paperwork, and fees.

Complete the course promptly after filing and confirm that the court has received the required proof.

### Unpaid court filing fees

If the court allows installment payments, those payments still must be completed. Required court filing fees generally must be paid in full before discharge unless the court has waived them.

Do not confuse permission to pay in installments with a fee waiver.

### Unresolved questions or missing records

The trustee may request documents, ask additional questions, or continue the creditors’ meeting.

A continued meeting does not, by itself, automatically prevent discharge. But unresolved issues may lead to a request to extend an objection deadline, an objection to discharge, a motion to dismiss, or another proceeding that affects timing.

Respond promptly to requests. If a document is unavailable or you cannot meet a deadline, address the problem rather than ignoring it.

### Certain reaffirmation proceedings

A [reaffirmation agreement](/library/reaffirmation-retain-redeem) preserves personal liability for a debt that would otherwise be discharged. Reaffirmation decisions should be made carefully, but an unresolved agreement does not automatically delay every discharge.

Specific proceedings can hold up entry—for example, a pending request to extend the deadline for filing a reaffirmation agreement or an unresolved hearing about whether an agreement creates an undue hardship. A debtor may also ask the court to delay discharge while a reaffirmation issue is addressed.

The effect depends on what has been filed and what the court has ordered.

## Objections and other disputes

A challenge to your **overall discharge** is different from a dispute about **one particular debt**.

An objection to overall discharge can prevent entry while the objection is resolved. Grounds can include concealment, false statements, inadequate financial records, failure to explain a loss of assets, or a prior discharge within the applicable [waiting period](/library/how-often-can-you-file). An allegation is not a court finding, but it requires a timely response.

A dispute about whether one debt is dischargeable generally does not, by itself, prevent discharge of other eligible debts. The court can enter the general discharge while litigation over that particular debt continues.

The 60-day deadline applies to certain dischargeability complaints, including specified fraud-related claims. It does not apply to every question about whether a [debt survives bankruptcy](/library/which-debts-does-bankruptcy-erase).

A pending motion to dismiss under § 707 can also delay discharge. This may involve the [means test](/library/what-is-the-means-test), the overall financial circumstances, or other grounds. Certain requests to extend deadlines can prevent entry while those requests are pending.

If you receive an objection, motion, or court notice, do not assume it will resolve itself. Read it promptly and address any response or hearing deadline.

## Discharge versus case closure

**Discharge addresses your personal liability for eligible debts. Case closure means the court’s administration of the case is complete.**

In a straightforward no-asset case, closure may occur around the time of discharge. The precise timing varies.

In an asset case, the trustee may still need to sell property, recover funds, review creditor claims, or distribute money. That work can take months or longer. Asset administration alone does not ordinarily require delaying discharge.

An open case does not mean your discharge failed. It does mean you must continue cooperating and comply with applicable document requests, turnover obligations, agreements, and court orders.

## The bottom line

A straightforward Chapter 7 discharge typically arrives about three to four months after filing, or 60 to 90 days after the first date set for the creditors’ meeting.

Your part is to complete debtor education, confirm completion is documented, pay required court fees unless waived, and respond to requests and notices.

If the expected discharge has not appeared, check the case docket or ask your attorney what remains outstanding. The important question is whether you are waiting for routine processing, an unmet requirement, or a proceeding that needs to be resolved.
