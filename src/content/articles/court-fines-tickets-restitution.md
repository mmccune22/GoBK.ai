## Criminal fines and restitution generally survive

Bankruptcy generally does not eliminate criminal fines or restitution imposed as part of a criminal sentence.

In Chapter 7, the Supreme Court has recognized strong protection against discharge for obligations imposed as part of a state criminal sentence. Federal criminal restitution also has a specific statutory exception.

A completed Chapter 13 plan likewise does not discharge restitution or a criminal fine included in a sentence following conviction. Paying part of the obligation through a plan does not erase the unpaid balance.

Bankruptcy also does not overturn the conviction, cancel probation conditions, or excuse compliance with criminal-court orders.

## Civil penalties can receive different treatment

Chapter 7 generally does not discharge a fine, penalty, or forfeiture that is payable to and for the benefit of a governmental unit and is not compensation for an actual financial loss, subject to statutory exceptions.

A discharge earned by completing Chapter 13 can eliminate some civil government penalties that would survive Chapter 7. But other exceptions may still apply.

For example, certain civil awards for restitution or damages arising from willful or malicious conduct causing personal injury or death survive a completed Chapter 13 plan.

A Chapter 13 [hardship discharge](/library/what-if-i-cant-finish-chapter-13) has narrower coverage than the discharge available after completing payments.

The question is therefore not simply whether a debt came from a court. It is what the obligation represents and which discharge rules apply.

## Traffic tickets, parking charges, and tolls

These obligations require attention to state law and the legal basis for the charge.

A traffic offense may be criminal in one jurisdiction and civil in another. An unpaid toll account may include the original toll, administrative charges, collection costs, and penalties, with different treatment potentially applying to each component.

A private company collecting the bill does not automatically make it ordinary private debt. The company may be collecting for a government agency. Conversely, not every charge connected to driving is a government penalty.

Review the actual citation, judgment, account breakdown, and governing law rather than relying on the name printed on a collection letter.

Court costs and related fees also require separate analysis. They should not automatically be grouped with either dischargeable consumer debts or nondischargeable criminal fines.

## Can bankruptcy help restore a license?

Sometimes, but the reason for the suspension matters.

Federal bankruptcy law generally prohibits a government agency from denying, suspending, or refusing to renew a license (the same [anti-discrimination rule](/library/is-bankruptcy-public) that protects government employees) solely because someone filed bankruptcy or failed to pay a debt that is dischargeable in the case.

That protection can matter when a license suspension is based on an unpaid civil accident judgment. It does not mean every accident judgment is dischargeable or that every related suspension must end.

A suspension based on points, unsafe driving, a criminal conviction, failure to maintain insurance, or unpaid support presents different issues. Bankruptcy does not automatically remove those grounds.

Even when bankruptcy addresses the debt causing the suspension, reinstatement may require submitting documents and satisfying other lawful requirements. Do not resume driving until the licensing agency confirms that your privilege has been restored.

## What happens to enforcement during bankruptcy?

Dischargeability and stay protection are separate questions. A debt may survive discharge while some collection activity is temporarily stayed. Other proceedings may continue despite the bankruptcy.

The [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay) generally does not stop criminal proceedings. Government police or regulatory actions and specified [support-enforcement measures](/library/child-support-alimony-and-bankruptcy) also have exceptions.

**A Chapter 13 plan that includes fines or restitution does not guarantee that criminal enforcement, probation proceedings, or license restrictions will stop.** The particular action and applicable law must be evaluated.

Do not ignore a hearing, payment order, or court deadline because a bankruptcy case has been filed.

## What bankruptcy can still accomplish

Even when a fine or restitution survives, [eliminating eligible credit cards, medical bills, and personal loans](/library/which-debts-does-bankruptcy-erase) may make the remaining obligation easier to afford.

Chapter 13 may also provide a structure for paying certain claims, but plan treatment, payment priority, and continuing enforcement rights matter. Any unpaid nondischargeable balance remains due afterward.

A payment arrangement or other relief through the court or agency may need to be pursued separately.

## The bottom line

Court-related debts need to be classified carefully: criminal or civil, punitive or compensatory, and subject to which discharge exception.

Chapter 13 can offer broader relief for some civil penalties, but criminal fines and restitution generally remain. License restoration and ongoing enforcement require their own analysis.

Bankruptcy may still improve the overall financial picture even when it cannot eliminate the court obligation itself.
