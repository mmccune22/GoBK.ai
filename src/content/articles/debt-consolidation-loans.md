## When consolidation can help

A consolidation loan may be useful when it lowers borrowing costs, establishes a realistic payoff schedule, and fits your budget.

For example, replacing high-interest credit card balances with a substantially lower fixed-rate personal loan can reduce interest. But the advertised rate alone does not establish savings.

Compare:

- The annual percentage rate, including applicable finance charges.
- Origination fees and any other costs.
- The amount actually available to pay existing debts.
- The monthly payment and number of payments.
- The total amount you will repay.
- Whether the rate is fixed or variable.
- Any collateral or co-signer requirements.

If a fee is deducted from the proceeds, a $20,000 loan may provide less than $20,000 to pay your creditors. Make sure the proposed loan actually covers the debts it is supposed to replace.

A longer term may provide needed payment relief, but that benefit should be weighed against the total cost. A smaller monthly payment is not automatically a cheaper loan.

## The offer you qualify for matters

An advertised rate may be available only to applicants with stronger credit or other qualifications.

Compare the written offer you can actually obtain—not the example used in the advertisement. If the rate and fees offer little improvement, consolidation may simply move the same problem to a new account.

Also check what the company is selling. Some advertisements use “consolidation” to describe [debt settlement](/library/debt-settlement-how-it-works) rather than a loan. If the proposal involves stopping payments and accumulating money for future negotiations, it is a different product with different risks.

## Balance transfers: useful terms can expire

A promotional balance-transfer offer can reduce interest temporarily, but calculate whether you can repay the transferred balance before the promotion ends.

Account for the transfer fee, promotional deadline, regular rate afterward, and required minimum payments. The minimum payment may be far below what is needed to finish during the promotional period.

New purchases can also receive different treatment from the transferred balance. Depending on the agreement, carrying a promotional transfer may cause purchases to accrue interest even when the transfer itself has a zero-percent rate.

Do not assume another promotional offer will be available when the first one expires.

## Using your home changes the risk

A home-equity loan or line of credit may offer a lower rate, but it gives the lender rights in your house.

Ordinary unsecured credit card debt does not give the issuer a consensual mortgage lien. Replacing it with home-secured borrowing creates a direct foreclosure risk if you cannot maintain the new obligation.

That distinction matters if bankruptcy later becomes necessary. Eligible personal liability may be discharged, but a valid home-equity lien generally survives unless legally addressed.

An unsecured creditor can still sue and may obtain a judgment lien under applicable law, so unsecured debt is not harmless. The point is that pledging your home changes the creditor’s rights and puts additional equity at risk.

Before using home equity to pay unsecured debt, compare options that do not require putting the house on the line.

## Retirement-plan loans have separate consequences

A 401(k) loan is not the same as an ordinary consolidation loan. It draws on retirement assets and is governed by the plan’s repayment rules.

Missed repayments or an employment change can lead to a taxable distribution or loan offset. The result is not always an immediate tax bill: qualifying offsets may have rollover options and extended deadlines. But replacing the offset amount can require money you do not have.

You also need to consider the effect on retirement savings and the [protection those funds may otherwise receive in bankruptcy](/library/retirement-accounts-in-bankruptcy).

Do not treat borrowing from your retirement account as risk-free merely because the interest is paid back into the plan.

## A co-signer takes on real liability

A co-signer generally becomes legally responsible for repayment. If you cannot pay, the lender may pursue that person under the agreement and applicable law.

Your later bankruptcy generally does not eliminate the co-signer’s obligation. A lower rate obtained through a relative’s credit therefore comes with a meaningful transfer of risk—not simply help completing an application.

## Prevent the old balances from returning

Consolidation needs a plan for the accounts being paid off.

That may mean restricting access, removing stored card details, changing spending arrangements, or closing selected accounts. Closing every card is not a universal requirement and can affect credit utilization, but leaving credit available without addressing an ongoing budget deficit can recreate the debt.

Not every deficit comes from discretionary spending. Unstable income, medical costs, caregiving, or housing expenses may be the underlying problem. A new loan does not resolve those pressures by itself.

If the new payment is affordable only because you expect to keep borrowing for basic expenses, reconsider whether consolidation provides a workable exit.

## Compare alternatives before signing

A [creditor hardship program](/library/alternatives-to-bankruptcy) or [debt management plan](/library/debt-management-plans) may reduce interest without a new loan. Settlement may reduce a balance but brings different collection and tax issues. Bankruptcy may eliminate or reorganize eligible obligations, subject to its own requirements and consequences.

Compare the total cost, repayment period, property risks, and what happens if your circumstances worsen. Approval for a loan is not proof that it is the right solution.

## The bottom line

Consolidation can help when the actual terms reduce costs or provide worthwhile payment relief within a sustainable plan.

It can also make the problem larger by extending repayment, adding fees, exposing collateral, or leaving room for old card balances to return.

Before signing, establish what the loan will cost, what it puts at risk, and whether your budget supports paying it off without borrowing again.
