## Counseling and a repayment plan are different services

Credit counseling generally starts with reviewing your income, expenses, and debts. A counselor may help you build a budget, understand repayment options, or identify other assistance.

That review does not obligate you to enroll in a debt management plan, commonly called a **DMP**. A useful counseling session should examine whether a plan fits—not simply lead to a sales pitch.

Initial counseling may be free, while plan administration may involve setup and monthly fees. Ask for the full fee schedule and whether reductions or waivers are available.

## How a debt management plan works

Under a typical DMP, you send a regular payment to the agency, which distributes it to participating creditors.

Creditors may agree to reduced interest, waived fees, or other repayment concessions. You generally repay the principal rather than negotiate a reduced balance. Completion often takes several years.

The actual terms depend on each creditor’s agreement. Do not assume every interest rate will become single-digit, every fee will disappear, or every creditor will participate.

Before starting, obtain a written schedule showing:

- Which accounts are included.
- Each creditor’s agreed terms.
- Your payment, including agency fees.
- When the agency will send payments.
- The expected payoff date.
- What happens if you miss a payment or leave the plan.

Confirm when creditor concessions take effect. Do not stop making existing payments merely because you submitted an application.

## Which debts can it address?

DMPs commonly handle unsecured debts such as credit cards and some medical or personal-loan balances.

Mortgages, car loans, taxes, and student loans may require separate arrangements. An agency may offer guidance about those obligations without including them in its DMP.

An account in collections or already involved in litigation requires specific review. Do not assume it is automatically excluded—or that enrollment stops the case.

Keep paying obligations outside the plan as required, and include them when testing whether the proposed DMP payment is affordable.

## What you gain—and what you do not

A suitable plan can simplify payments, reduce interest costs, and establish a repayment schedule without taking out another loan.

But it does not discharge debt or create an automatic stay. Any agreement to limit collection depends on the participating creditor’s terms.

A DMP also does not erase accurate late-payment history. Accounts may be closed or restricted, affecting available credit and potentially your score. Reporting practices and outcomes vary; neither “no credit impact” nor a guaranteed score improvement is a reliable promise.

If delinquent accounts are brought current under an agreed arrangement, that does not authorize changing the original delinquency date to improperly extend negative reporting.

## Who it fits

A DMP may work when you have reliable income and can repay the principal if interest and fees are reduced.

The budget must cover more than the proposed monthly payment. Account for housing, food, insurance, transportation, taxes, support, and reasonably predictable irregular expenses.

Ask what would happen after a job interruption or a major repair. If the plan works only when nothing goes wrong for several years, it may be too fragile.

If you cannot realistically repay the principal even with concessions, compare other options. Bankruptcy may offer relief, but [Chapter 7 eligibility](/library/what-is-the-means-test), property risks, and [Chapter 13 payment requirements](/library/chapter-13-monthly-payment) need their own analysis. Do not assume Chapter 13 always pays unsecured creditors only a small fraction.

## Vetting the agency

Before enrolling:

- Verify the agency’s identity, nonprofit status if claimed, and any required state licensing or registration.
- Review counselor qualifications and any claimed accreditation or association membership.
- Check complaint information with your state attorney general or consumer protection agency.
- Obtain written fees, refund terms, cancellation rights, and creditor arrangements.
- Ask how funds are handled and how you can verify distributions.

Membership in a professional association can be useful information, but it is not a guarantee of quality or suitability.

Likewise, appearing on the U.S. Trustee Program’s [approved list](/library/credit-counseling-and-debtor-education) means an agency is approved to provide specified bankruptcy-related counseling. It is not a government recommendation of that agency or an endorsement of a particular DMP.

Be cautious about pressure to enroll immediately, guaranteed results, or a proposal made without a meaningful budget review.

## Monitor the plan after enrollment

Continue reviewing creditor statements. Confirm that payments arrive on time and that promised concessions appear.

A payment to the agency does not establish that every creditor received the correct amount. Report discrepancies promptly and retain records.

If the payment becomes unaffordable, ask what changes are possible and what happens to concessions if you leave. Do not assume the plan can pause without consequences.

## The bottom line

A debt management plan can help when lower interest and organized payments make full repayment realistic.

Its value depends on the actual creditor agreements, total fees, and a budget you can maintain. Counseling should help determine whether the plan fits—not substitute for that decision.

Compare it with direct hardship arrangements, [settlement](/library/debt-settlement-how-it-works), and bankruptcy using the same questions: total cost, time to completion, collection exposure, and what happens if the plan fails.
