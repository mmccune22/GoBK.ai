## First, confirm what the creditor is collecting

A bankruptcy discharge generally prohibits attempts to collect covered debts as your personal liability. Selling the account or assigning a new collector does not remove that protection.

But receiving a communication after discharge does not automatically establish a violation. Determine whether it concerns:

- A debt [covered by the discharge](/library/can-a-discharged-debt-come-back).
- A nondischargeable obligation, such as qualifying [support](/library/child-support-alimony-and-bankruptcy) or certain [taxes](/library/can-old-tax-debt-be-discharged).
- An enforceable [reaffirmation agreement](/library/reaffirmation-retain-redeem).
- A surviving lien on a house, vehicle, or other property.
- A new obligation arising after filing.

A lender may retain rights against collateral even when your personal liability was discharged. Some informational statements concerning those rights may be permitted. Conversely, a disclaimer does not automatically make a demand for discharged personal liability lawful.

## Why collection may continue

Sometimes bankruptcy information was not correctly processed or passed to a new collector. Other cases involve disputed discharge coverage or collection practices that disregard the discharge.

You do not need to determine the collector’s motive before documenting the problem. But intent and the reasonableness of its position can matter when a court considers sanctions.

The important questions are what the discharge covered, what the creditor did, and what information it had.

## The practical steps

### 1. Preserve the evidence

Keep letters, emails, text messages, voicemails, and screenshots. Record call dates, the company’s name, the account involved, and what was requested.

Save evidence of payments, withdrawals, fees, or other losses. If the communication threatens a lawsuit, garnishment, or repossession, note the stated deadline.

### 2. Locate your bankruptcy records

Find the discharge order, case number, filing date, and relevant account information.

Additional documents may matter, including reaffirmation agreements, lien orders, or a ruling about a particular debt. The discharge order usually does not identify every covered account individually.

### 3. Notify your bankruptcy attorney promptly

You do not need to wait through repeated demands before reporting the issue.

If appropriate, a written response can identify the case and discharge and explain that the collector is seeking payment of discharged personal liability. Provide relevant documentation and keep a copy.

Verify the recipient before sharing information, and avoid sending unnecessary sensitive records. Do not make a payment or sign a new agreement merely to stop the calls before determining whether anything remains enforceable.

### 4. Do not ignore court papers

A lawsuit seeking discharged personal liability may violate the discharge injunction. It still requires prompt attention.

Make sure the discharge issue is raised through the appropriate process and that response deadlines are addressed. A complaint to a regulator or a letter to the collector does not necessarily extend a court deadline.

## What the bankruptcy court can do

The bankruptcy court can enforce its discharge order, including through civil contempt in appropriate circumstances. A closed case may need to be reopened to address the issue; reopening does not itself undo the discharge.

Under the Supreme Court’s decision in *Taggart v. Lorenzen*, civil contempt may be appropriate when there is no fair ground of doubt that the discharge barred the conduct. A creditor’s claimed good faith does not automatically protect objectively unreasonable collection.

Available relief can include compensation for proven losses and appropriate attorney fees or other sanctions. The standard, evidence, and controlling law matter. **Neither an award nor free legal representation is guaranteed.**

A collection letter should therefore be evaluated rather than treated as an automatic damages claim.

## The credit-reporting version

Review your [credit reports](/library/when-does-bankruptcy-fall-off-credit-report) if a discharged account appears to remain active or collectible.

For an ordinary unsecured debt whose liability was discharged, an outstanding balance or new past-due amounts may be inaccurate. But accurate prebankruptcy payment history can remain, and surviving or reaffirmed obligations require different treatment.

A recent “updated” date is not necessarily improper re-aging. Check whether the original delinquency date or reporting period has been changed incorrectly.

Dispute specific inaccuracies with both the credit bureau and the company furnishing the information. Include supporting documents and keep copies of your submissions and responses.

An inaccurate report does not automatically establish a discharge violation. Credit-reporting duties and discharge enforcement are separate legal issues, although the same conduct can sometimes implicate both.

## If the problem continues

Follow up on dispute results and preserve any new collection attempts.

A CFPB complaint may help address a debt-collection or reporting problem, but it does not replace necessary court action or guarantee compensation. If the creditor continues seeking discharged personal liability, further bankruptcy-court relief may be appropriate.

## The bottom line

A discharge is enforceable protection, not a request that creditors may disregard.

Confirm what the debt and communication involve, keep the evidence, and address apparent violations promptly. Do not assume an old balance is payable simply because someone demands it—and do not ignore a lawsuit because you believe the discharge resolves it.

The goal is to stop improper collection, correct inaccurate reporting, and obtain any relief justified by the facts.
