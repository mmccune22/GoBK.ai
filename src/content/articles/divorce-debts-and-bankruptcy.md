## Start by identifying the obligation

A divorce can create several different financial obligations:

- Child support or alimony.
- A payment dividing marital property.
- A requirement to pay a joint credit card, mortgage, or other debt.
- A promise to reimburse or protect a former spouse if a creditor collects from them.
- Attorney fees or other court-ordered payments.

These obligations do not necessarily receive the same bankruptcy treatment. One provision can also create more than one legal relationship—for example, your debt to a bank and your separate obligation to your former spouse.

## Support survives bankruptcy

Qualifying domestic support obligations are not discharged in Chapter 7 or Chapter 13.

The bankruptcy analysis looks at whether the obligation functions as support. A decree’s label matters, but it does not necessarily control. Depending on the circumstances, obligations involving housing payments, custody-related attorney fees, or other expenses may qualify as support even without that label.

Likewise, calling a payment “alimony” does not automatically settle its classification if its actual function is a division of property.

Support payments must be addressed separately from ordinary dischargeable debts. See [What happens to child support and alimony in bankruptcy?](/library/child-support-alimony-and-bankruptcy) for payment requirements and enforcement rules.

## Property-settlement debts: where the chapters differ

Certain nonsupport debts owed to a spouse, former spouse, or child and incurred through a divorce or separation generally survive Chapter 7.

Examples may include an equalization payment or a qualifying obligation to pay a joint debt and hold a former spouse harmless.

After successful completion of Chapter 13, those obligations may be discharged because the completed-plan discharge does not include the same divorce-debt exception. Other discharge exceptions can still apply.

A Chapter 13 [hardship discharge](/library/what-if-i-cant-finish-chapter-13) is narrower and generally does not eliminate these obligations.

Not everything in a divorce decree is simply a debt that bankruptcy can erase. An ownership interest already awarded to a former spouse, a retirement-plan division, or a lien securing an obligation can require separate analysis.

## A joint debt can create two separate obligations

Suppose a divorce decree says:

“You must pay the joint credit card and hold your former spouse harmless.”

There may be two obligations:

- **The credit card debt owed to the issuer.**
- **The obligation owed to your former spouse under the decree.**

If you discharge your personal liability to the credit card issuer, the issuer generally may still collect from your former spouse if they remain legally responsible.

In Chapter 7, your separate qualifying obligation to protect or reimburse your former spouse generally survives. Your former spouse may therefore retain an enforceable claim against you even though the bank can no longer collect from you personally.

A completed Chapter 13 plan may discharge that nonsupport obligation to your former spouse, but it generally does not eliminate the issuer’s rights against them.

A divorce decree assigning a debt to one spouse ordinarily does not change the original creditor’s contract. The same distinction matters when your former spouse was ordered to pay a debt for which you remain liable.

## Mortgages, car loans, and liens still matter

Being awarded the house or car in a divorce does not necessarily remove the other spouse from the loan. Nor does discharging personal liability ordinarily remove the lender’s lien.

A decree may require refinancing, selling property, making payments, or reimbursing a former spouse. Each requirement needs to be evaluated separately.

Discharging a payment obligation also does not automatically transfer title, undo a completed property division, or remove a lien created by the decree.

The practical question is both who owes money and who owns—or has enforceable rights in—the property.

## Should bankruptcy happen before or after divorce?

There is no universally better sequence.

Married spouses sometimes can [file a joint bankruptcy](/library/married-filing-alone-or-together) before the divorce, potentially addressing shared debts in one case. But a joint filing does not automatically double every [exemption](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions), eliminate every joint debt, or make shared representation appropriate.

Income, property rights, conflicts between spouses, the proposed divorce terms, and each person’s eligibility can change the analysis. Separate filings may be necessary or preferable.

During a divorce, bankruptcy can also affect scheduling. The [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay) generally allows dissolution of the marriage and support proceedings to continue, while division of property belonging to the bankruptcy estate may require relief from the stay.

The bankruptcy and divorce work should be coordinated before signing agreements, transferring property, or assuming one proceeding will resolve the other.

## The bottom line

Divorce debts require more than a list of balances. Identify what each obligation does, who can enforce it, and whether it involves support, personal liability, ownership, or a lien.

Support survives. Qualifying nonsupport divorce debts generally survive Chapter 7 but may be discharged after completing Chapter 13. Joint creditors usually retain their rights against a former spouse who remains liable.

The right approach accounts for the decree and the original debts together, so relief from one obligation does not leave an unexpected obligation elsewhere.
