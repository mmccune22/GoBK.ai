## The basic deal

You propose a repayment plan and make regular payments to a Chapter 13 [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee), who distributes the money to creditors according to the approved plan. Some obligations, such as an ongoing mortgage payment, may be paid directly rather than through the trustee.

Filing generally triggers the [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay), which stops most collection activity, including garnishments, foreclosure proceedings, and repossessions. There are exceptions, and prior bankruptcy filings can limit that protection. A creditor can also ask the court for permission to resume collection against particular property.

Unlike Chapter 7, Chapter 13 generally does not involve a trustee selling your property. You can often keep property that would be at risk in Chapter 7, but keeping it may increase what your plan must pay. You must also meet the payment requirements for a house, car, or other property securing a loan.

After you complete the plan and meet the other discharge requirements, the court eliminates your obligation to pay remaining eligible debts. Some debts survive, including an ongoing home mortgage, child support, and certain taxes and student loans.

## The myth to clear up first

**Chapter 13 does not necessarily require you to repay all your debt.**

Some plans pay unsecured creditors in full. Others pay only a portion of credit card balances, medical bills, and other eligible unsecured debts, with the unpaid balance discharged after successful completion.

[Your payment](/library/chapter-13-monthly-payment) is not simply whatever you feel comfortable paying. The calculation considers your income and allowed expenses, debts that must be paid through the plan, and the value creditors would receive if you filed Chapter 7. Those requirements can create a minimum payment even when your budget is tight.

A workable plan must satisfy the legal requirements and leave you able to make the payments.

## Who can file

Chapter 13 is for individuals with regular income. You do not need a traditional paycheck: self-employment income, Social Security, disability benefits, pensions, and other reliable sources may support a plan. In some circumstances, dependable contributions from a household or family member can help.

The question is whether the available income is stable enough to fund the proposed payments.

Chapter 13 also has debt limits. As of November 2026, qualifying noncontingent, liquidated debts must be below:

- $526,700 in unsecured debt.
- $1,580,125 in secured debt.

These limits generally adjust every three years, and Congress has considered proposals to raise them. Because these figures can change, confirm the limits in effect when you file. Corporations and LLCs cannot file Chapter 13, although an individual who owns a business may qualify. Other requirements, including prefiling [credit counseling](/library/credit-counseling-and-debtor-education) and restrictions arising from certain prior cases, also apply.

## Why choose Chapter 13?

Sometimes Chapter 7 is unavailable or would not provide the relief someone needs. For example, the [means-test](/library/what-is-the-means-test) calculation may show enough disposable income to make Chapter 7 inappropriate, or a [prior bankruptcy](/library/how-often-can-you-file) may prevent another Chapter 7 discharge.

[Being above your state’s median income](/library/above-median-income-now-what) does not, by itself, rule out Chapter 7. And the rules governing a new filing are different from the rules governing a new discharge.

Chapter 13 can also offer tools that Chapter 7 does not. Depending on the circumstances, a plan can:

- **[Help save a house](/library/chapter-13-stop-foreclosure-catch-up-mortgage).** Catch up on missed mortgage payments over time while maintaining ongoing payments.
- **[Restructure a car loan](/library/chapter-13-keep-car-cramdown).** Change repayment terms and, in qualifying cases, reduce the secured balance to the vehicle’s value.
- **Protect nonexempt property.** Keep property that could be sold in Chapter 7 by paying unsecured creditors at least what they would receive in that case.
- **Address certain tax debts.** Pay required tax claims over the plan rather than having to resolve them immediately. Interest, penalties, and tax liens require separate attention.
- **Discharge certain additional debts.** A completed Chapter 13 plan can discharge some obligations that Chapter 7 cannot, including certain divorce property-settlement debts. Child support and alimony remain protected from discharge.

## The shape of a case

You file a petition, financial schedules, and other required documents, along with a proposed repayment plan. You also pay a court filing fee.

Plan payments generally must begin within 30 days after filing, even though the court has not yet approved the plan. You cannot wait for approval before starting payments.

Early in the case, you attend a [meeting of creditors](/library/what-is-a-341-meeting), where the trustee asks questions about your finances and proposed plan. The trustee and creditors can raise objections, and the plan may need revisions before the judge approves it. That approval is called confirmation.

Once the plan is confirmed, you continue making the required payments and complying with its terms. You must also stay current on obligations that arise during the case, such as ongoing support payments and taxes. Your plan or local rules may require tax returns, income updates, or permission before taking on new debt.

The plan usually lasts three years for someone below the applicable state median income and five years for someone above it. Below-median filers may need a longer plan to make the required payments manageable. Early completion has separate rules, discussed in [Can I pay off my Chapter 13 plan early?](/library/chapter-13-early-payoff)

## The practical challenge

Three to five years is a substantial commitment. Income can drop, expenses can rise, and family circumstances can change.

A plan that becomes unaffordable does not always mean the case must fail. Depending on the circumstances, you may be able to modify payments, convert to Chapter 7, or qualify for a hardship discharge. None of those options is automatic, and a modification still has to meet legal requirements.

Tell your attorney promptly if you lose income, face a major expense, or fall behind. Addressing the problem early gives you more opportunity to evaluate the available options.

These situations are covered in [What happens if I can’t finish my Chapter 13 plan?](/library/what-if-i-cant-finish-chapter-13)

## The bottom line

Chapter 13 gives you time and a legal structure to deal with debts that may be difficult to resolve outside bankruptcy. It can be especially useful when you need to catch up on a mortgage, restructure a car loan, address certain taxes, or protect property at risk in Chapter 7.

The tradeoff is a repayment plan that lasts years and requires ongoing attention. The right plan must accomplish your goals, satisfy the law, and fit a budget you can realistically maintain. You may not have to repay everything, but you do need a workable path to completion.
