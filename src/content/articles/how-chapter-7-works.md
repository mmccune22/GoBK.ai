## The shape of a case

**Before filing.** Filing requires detailed information about your income, debts, expenses, property, and financial history. You also complete [credit counseling](/library/credit-counseling-and-debtor-education) through an approved provider within the 180 days before filing.

Your petition, schedules, and related forms describe your financial situation under oath. Review them carefully before signing, and correct anything inaccurate or incomplete.

**Filing day.** Filing creates your bankruptcy case. The [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay) generally takes effect immediately and stops most collection activity, including many collection lawsuits, garnishments, foreclosures, and repossessions. Exceptions apply, and prior bankruptcy filings can limit the stay.

**The meeting with the trustee.** Several weeks after filing, you attend the [341 meeting](/library/what-is-a-341-meeting) of creditors. The [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) asks questions under oath about your paperwork and finances. No judge attends. In a straightforward case, questioning often takes five to ten minutes, although additional questions or a continued meeting may be necessary.

**After the meeting.** Complete [debtor education](/library/credit-counseling-and-debtor-education), make sure completion is documented with the court, and respond to requests for records or explanations. Certain objections generally have a deadline 60 days after the first date set for the meeting. The trustee may continue investigating property or transactions beyond that period.

**The discharge.** If the requirements are satisfied and no unresolved issue prevents it, discharge generally arrives 60 to 90 days after the first date set for the meeting—typically about three to four months after filing. It removes your personal liability for dischargeable debts and prohibits creditors from collecting those debts from you personally.

Discharge and case closure are separate events. A simple case may close around the time of discharge. A case involving asset administration can remain open longer.

## What “liquidation” actually means

The Chapter 7 trustee reviews your financial affairs and determines whether property or recoverable funds are available to pay creditors.

That does not mean everything you own will be sold. [Exemptions](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions) protect qualifying property or a specified amount of value in it. Loans and liens also affect whether an asset has value available for creditors, as do ownership interests and the costs of a sale.

Most individual Chapter 7 cases are “no-asset” cases: the trustee makes no distribution to unsecured creditors. That describes the outcome for creditors, not whether the filer owns a home, car, furniture, or other belongings.

If property has meaningful value that is not protected, the trustee may sell it. Sometimes an agreement allows a filer to keep an asset by making a payment, but that is not guaranteed. Do not assume you can arrange a buyback after filing.

The trustee can also investigate certain transfers and payments made before bankruptcy and, where the law permits, recover money or property. These issues can arise even when the filer acted honestly.

If keeping nonexempt property is a priority, Chapter 13 may offer another approach. Its plan generally must pay unsecured creditors at least what they would receive in Chapter 7, and other requirements may mean paying more.

## Who Chapter 7 fits

Chapter 7 often works well for someone who:

- Qualifies under the applicable income and eligibility rules.
- Has substantial debts that Chapter 7 can discharge, such as credit cards, medical bills, and many personal loans.
- Has property that is protected or understands and accepts the potential loss of nonexempt property.
- Does not need a court-supervised repayment plan to catch up on secured debts.

Income below the applicable state median generally avoids the longer [means-test](/library/what-is-the-means-test) calculation. [Above-median income](/library/above-median-income-now-what) does not automatically rule out Chapter 7; some people qualify after allowed expenses are considered.

Passing the means test is not the entire eligibility analysis. Prior cases, current financial circumstances, and other legal requirements also matter.

## Its limits

**It does not provide a mortgage catch-up plan.** Chapter 7 may temporarily stop foreclosure, but it generally cannot force a lender to accept missed payments over several years. Keeping a home usually requires addressing the default and maintaining ongoing payments. Chapter 13 provides a [structured way to cure qualifying mortgage arrears](/library/chapter-13-stop-foreclosure-catch-up-mortgage).

**Keeping financed property requires more than a discharge.** A discharge generally does not remove a lender’s lien. A mortgage or car lender may retain rights against its collateral even when your personal liability is discharged. For financed cars, reaffirmation, redemption, surrender, and the possibility of keeping the car while paying are covered in [4.3](/library/reaffirmation-retain-redeem).

**[Some debts survive](/library/which-debts-does-bankruptcy-erase).** Child support, alimony, certain taxes, and many other obligations are not discharged. Student loans generally require a separate proceeding and a court determination that the applicable discharge standard is met.

**Prior discharges affect timing.** After receiving a Chapter 7 discharge, you generally must [wait eight years](/library/how-often-can-you-file) between filing dates to receive another Chapter 7 discharge. That is not an absolute ban on filing another bankruptcy case during those eight years. Different rules apply to other chapter combinations.

**New bills still need a plan.** Chapter 7 generally addresses debts arising before filing. It does not increase income or prevent new medical bills, living expenses, or other debts from accumulating afterward.

## The bottom line

Chapter 7 can provide relatively quick relief from eligible debts without years of plan payments. Most individual cases involve no distribution to unsecured creditors, but property exposure and debts that survive must be understood before filing.

Careful preparation, accurate disclosures, and continued cooperation help the process move forward. For a straightforward case, discharge typically arrives about three to four months after filing. Whether the case closes at the same time depends on what remains for the trustee to administer.
