## Start with what you actually own

When you file bankruptcy, your legal and equitable interests in property generally become part of the bankruptcy estate.

For jointly owned property, that usually means your interest—not automatically the entire asset. But do not assume two names mean equal ownership. Your share depends on the ownership documents, applicable state law, and sometimes evidence about contributions or agreements.

Joint bank accounts require particular attention. The right to withdraw money and the legal ownership of that money are not necessarily the same thing. Account records and the source of deposits can help establish whose funds are involved.

Marital community property follows different rules (see also [Can married people file bankruptcy alone?](/library/married-filing-alone-or-together)): qualifying community property can enter the bankruptcy estate even when only one spouse files.

## How exemptions protect your interest

Once your ownership interest is identified, the next question is how much value it has and whether an [exemption](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions) protects it.

For example, if you own half of a property worth $100,000 with no liens, your interest might start at approximately $50,000. The actual bankruptcy analysis can also involve ownership rights, valuation, sale costs, and the available exemptions.

If your interest is fully protected—or would produce no meaningful benefit for the estate—a Chapter 7 [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) generally has no reason to sell it.

But co-ownership alone is not an exemption. A house, vehicle, or account does not become protected simply because someone else’s name appears beside yours.

## When a Chapter 7 trustee can sell the whole property

For certain forms of co-ownership, the Bankruptcy Code allows a trustee to seek court approval to sell both the estate’s interest and the co-owner’s interest.

The trustee must meet specific requirements, including showing that dividing the property is impracticable, selling only the estate’s interest would produce significantly less, and the benefit to the estate outweighs the detriment to the co-owner.

The co-owner has rights in that process. These include an opportunity under the statute to purchase the property at the proposed sale price before the sale is completed. If a sale occurs, proceeds are distributed according to the parties’ interests after applicable costs and other required deductions.

A sale is not automatic whenever there is nonexempt equity. Sometimes an approved agreement allows a co-owner or another person to pay for the estate’s interest instead. But neither an agreement nor a particular outcome is guaranteed.

## How Chapter 13 can help

Chapter 13 can often protect jointly owned property that would face a sale risk in Chapter 7.

Instead of a trustee selling the asset, you propose a [repayment plan](/library/how-chapter-13-works). Unsecured creditors generally must receive at least what they would receive in a hypothetical Chapter 7 case, including any value attributable to your nonexempt property interest.

That does not necessarily mean paying the entire property’s value or the co-owner’s share. The calculation considers what would actually be available to creditors.

The plan must also satisfy other legal requirements and be affordable. Chapter 13 does not eliminate mortgage liens, resolve every ownership dispute, or prevent a co-owner from exercising all rights they may have under state law.

## Married owners: entireties and community property

### Tenancy by the entirety

Some states recognize a form of spousal ownership called **tenancy by the entirety**.

Depending on state law and the debts involved, it may protect qualifying property from creditors of only one spouse. Joint debts and special creditor rights can change the result.

Being married or owning a home together does not automatically establish entireties protection. The ownership form, applicable law, and exemption requirements matter.

### Community property

In community-property jurisdictions, the bankruptcy estate may include qualifying interests of both spouses in community property, even when only one files.

The analysis therefore cannot always be reduced to “my half goes in; my spouse’s half stays out.” Separate property, community property, and responsibility for particular debts must be evaluated under the applicable rules.

## Names on titles matter—but they are not the whole answer

Your name might appear on a parent’s bank account for convenience or on a vehicle used and paid for by a relative. Those arrangements need to be disclosed and explained; an informal statement that something is “not really mine” may not resolve the ownership question.

The reverse matters too. You may have an ownership interest even when your name does not appear on the title, depending on applicable law and the facts.

Relevant information can include deeds, account agreements, purchase records, payment histories, trusts, and written ownership agreements. Bankruptcy looks at legal and equitable rights, not simply what a family calls the arrangement.

Do not remove your name, give away your interest, or move money to someone else merely to keep it out of bankruptcy. Transfers can be reviewed and potentially reversed, and concealment can jeopardize a discharge. Disclose existing arrangements and prior transfers accurately.

## The bottom line

Joint ownership does not automatically put the entire asset at risk, and it does not automatically protect it.

The key questions are what interest you own, how much value is available after liens and applicable deductions, and which exemptions protect that value. In Chapter 7, a trustee may sometimes sell the entire property. Chapter 13 may provide a way to retain it through required plan payments.

Clear ownership records and complete disclosure help identify the risks before a filing affects you or a co-owner.
