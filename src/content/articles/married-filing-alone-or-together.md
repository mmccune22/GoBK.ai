## The ground rules

Marriage does not automatically make every debt joint. Responsibility depends on the account, the type of debt, and state law. A debt in one spouse’s name may belong only to that spouse, but some state laws create responsibility for certain family expenses even without both spouses signing.

Being an authorized user on a spouse’s credit card is also different from being a joint borrower. Permission to use the card generally does not, by itself, make you responsible for the balance.

Either spouse can file alone. Neither can require the other to join a bankruptcy case.

A solo filing still requires a look at the household’s finances:

**Your spouse’s income may count.** When spouses live together, the non-filing spouse’s income generally must be disclosed and considered in the [means test](/library/what-is-the-means-test). A “marital adjustment” may exclude amounts not used for the filing spouse’s or dependents’ household expenses. It is not an automatic deduction for every separate expense or debt the other spouse has. Different rules may apply when spouses are separated.

**Your discharge generally protects only you.** If both spouses are responsible for a credit card, personal loan, or other debt, one spouse’s discharge generally does not release the other spouse. The creditor can still seek payment from the non-filing spouse.

**Jointly owned property still matters.** Your ownership interest in a home, vehicle, bank account, or other shared property must be disclosed and evaluated. Filing alone does not automatically keep that property out of the case or protect the other spouse’s interest from every possible consequence.

**Community-property states have additional rules.** Certain community property can become part of the bankruptcy estate even when only one spouse files. A discharge may also protect certain community property from collection without eliminating the non-filing spouse’s personal liability. These rules can substantially change the analysis.

## When together makes sense

Joint filing often makes sense when both spouses have substantial debts they need to discharge. That includes debts they owe together and separate debts each spouse owes individually.

A joint case uses one petition and carries one court filing fee. Both spouses must provide the required information, complete their individual education requirements, and participate in the case. Filing together is often less expensive than filing two separate cases, although attorney fees vary.

The main benefit is addressing both spouses’ eligible debts at the same time. If both spouses receive a discharge of a joint credit card debt, the creditor generally cannot pursue either spouse personally for that discharged balance.

Property protections also matter. Some [exemptions](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions) are available separately to each spouse, but filing together does not automatically double every exemption. The result depends on the applicable law, ownership, and type of property.

Both spouses must qualify for the relief they seek. Filing jointly does not make an otherwise nondischargeable debt disappear.

## When filing alone makes sense

Filing alone may be enough when most or all of the debt belongs to one spouse and the other spouse has little debt requiring bankruptcy relief.

For example, one spouse might have substantial credit card or business debt while the other has no personal responsibility for it. A solo filing may resolve the debt problem without requiring both spouses to file.

The non-filing spouse does not become a bankruptcy filer simply because their spouse files. But that does not guarantee their credit will remain unaffected. Missed payments, shared accounts, and debts they still owe can continue to affect their credit.

In Chapter 13, a co-debtor stay can temporarily protect a non-filing spouse from collection on certain shared consumer debts while the plan is active. Chapter 7 does not offer that protection.

Filing alone can also make sense when one spouse is not eligible for a discharge, does not want to file, or has financial circumstances that make a joint filing less useful.

The important question is what remains afterward. If a solo filing leaves the other spouse responsible for substantial joint debt, the household may still face much of the same financial pressure.

## The bottom line

You do not have to file bankruptcy just because your spouse does, and you do not have to file together simply because you are married.

Start by separating debts into three groups: debts you owe, debts your spouse owes, and debts you owe together. Then consider household income, property ownership, available exemptions, and what obligations would remain after filing.

The goal is to choose the approach that gives your household useful relief while accounting for the consequences to both spouses.
