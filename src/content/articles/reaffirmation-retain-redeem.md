## First, separate the car’s equity from the loan

There are two different questions: whether the car is protected from the bankruptcy [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee), and what you must do to keep it from the lender.

**Equity** is generally the car’s value minus the balance of valid liens against it. [Exemptions](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions) may protect some or all of your interest. If substantial equity is not protected, the trustee may sell the car even if you are current on payments.

The lender’s rights are a separate issue. Protecting your equity with an exemption does not eliminate the lender’s lien. Likewise, signing a reaffirmation agreement does not protect nonexempt equity from the trustee.

If you own the car outright, there is no loan to reaffirm or redeem. The main question is whether your ownership interest is protected.

## Reaffirmation: keeping personal responsibility for the loan

A reaffirmation agreement is an agreement with the lender that keeps an otherwise dischargeable debt enforceable against you personally. It must satisfy bankruptcy-law requirements; signing and mailing a form alone does not necessarily make it effective.

The consequence matters. If you later default and the lender repossesses and sells the car, you may still owe the remaining balance, subject to applicable law. A breakdown or loss of the vehicle does not automatically cancel a reaffirmed debt.

Reaffirmation may make sense when keeping the car depends on it and the obligation is affordable. Consider:

- The balance compared with the car’s value.
- The interest rate and remaining loan term.
- The monthly payment, insurance, and likely repair costs.
- Whether the lender will offer better terms.
- What happens if your income falls or the car stops working.

You can request a lower balance, rate, or payment, but the lender does not have to agree. Reaffirmation also does not guarantee improved credit or a particular credit-reporting practice.

Some agreements require court approval or a hearing. Requirements depend on matters such as attorney involvement and whether the agreement creates a presumption of undue hardship. Do not assume a hearing will occur or that the court will independently evaluate every agreement.

**There is a limited right to cancel.** You may rescind a reaffirmation agreement by notifying the creditor before discharge or within 60 days after the agreement is filed with the court, whichever is later. Keep proof of that notice. Canceling the agreement does not itself guarantee that you can keep the car.

## Redemption: paying a qualifying car’s value in a lump sum

Redemption can help when a car is worth substantially less than the loan balance.

For a qualifying vehicle, you pay the lender the amount of its allowed secured claim in one lump sum. That amount is generally based on the applicable replacement-value standard, capped by the secured debt—not simply a trade-in estimate or whatever price you choose.

For example, suppose you owe $14,000 and the legally determined redemption amount is $7,000. Paying the $7,000 can release the lender’s lien, while the remaining dischargeable balance is addressed through bankruptcy.

Redemption under § 722 generally applies to personal property intended primarily for personal, family, or household use, securing a dischargeable consumer debt. The property must be exempt or abandoned by the trustee. A business vehicle or business-purpose loan may not qualify.

The practical hurdle is funding the lump sum. Some people use available protected funds or family assistance. Others obtain redemption financing.

If you borrow, compare the full cost: interest, fees, monthly payment, and total repayment. A smaller principal balance does not automatically make a new loan affordable. The new financing is a new obligation that generally will not be discharged in the current case.

Redemption requires the appropriate court process. Do not assume a valuation estimate or informal discussion with the lender completes it.

## Keeping the car and paying without reaffirming: understand the risk

Some lenders accept ongoing payments and allow borrowers to keep their cars without an enforceable reaffirmation agreement. This is sometimes called “retain and pay” or “ride-through.”

**It is not a universally available right, and staying current does not always prevent repossession.**

Bankruptcy law imposes requirements and deadlines for stating and carrying out your intentions concerning financed personal property. Failing to meet applicable requirements can end bankruptcy protections for the car and allow the lender to exercise rights available under nonbankruptcy law.

The outcome can depend on the contract, applicable law, lender practices, and what happened during the reaffirmation process. A lender accepting payments is not necessarily promising that it will never repossess.

If personal liability on the loan is discharged and no enforceable reaffirmation preserves it, the lender generally cannot collect a later deficiency from you personally. But its lien remains, and the car may still be at risk.

That distinction is essential: **protection from personal collection is different from protection against losing the car.**

Do not treat paying without reaffirming as the default choice simply because it worked for someone else. Before relying on it, understand the specific lender’s position and the legal consequences in your case.

## Surrender: giving up the car and addressing the debt

Sometimes keeping the car is not financially workable. A high payment, expensive repairs, or a large loan balance may make surrender the better option.

Surrender means making the car available to the lender rather than trying to keep it. If the loan debt is dischargeable and you do not enter an enforceable reaffirmation agreement, the remaining personal liability generally is discharged, including a qualifying deficiency after sale.

Surrender does not necessarily mean the lender picks up the car immediately. Coordinate the return and keep records. Until possession and ownership issues are resolved, address insurance, storage, registration, and related obligations rather than assuming they end when you list an intention to surrender.

Your discharge also does not release a [co-borrower or guarantor](/library/what-cant-bankruptcy-fix) from their own liability.

## The bottom line

Start with two questions: is the car’s equity protected, and what is required to keep it under the loan?

Reaffirmation preserves personal liability. Redemption can replace an underwater balance with a qualifying lump-sum payoff. Paying without reaffirming may work in some circumstances, but continued payments alone do not guarantee that you keep the car. Surrender may provide a more manageable fresh start when the vehicle is unaffordable.

Review these options early. Deadlines apply, and a reaffirmation agreement deserves careful consideration before you sign.
