## Start with financial stability

Rebuilding works best when new credit rests on a workable budget. Protect housing, food, insurance, transportation, and other essential expenses before taking on another payment.

If possible, begin a small emergency reserve. Savings do not directly raise your credit score, but they can help prevent an unexpected expense from becoming a missed payment or another expensive loan.

The timeline below is a flexible guide for the first year after discharge—not a requirement to open accounts on particular dates. If your case is still pending, especially in [Chapter 13](/library/how-chapter-13-works), check the applicable borrowing and approval requirements first.

## Months one to three: review and correct

Obtain reports from all three nationwide credit bureaus through [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/).

Review the [bankruptcy entry](/library/when-does-bankruptcy-fall-off-credit-report) and each affected account. Check whether the chapter, filing date, case status, balances, and payment history are accurate.

Ordinary unsecured debts whose liability was discharged generally should not appear as balances you currently owe or continue accumulating new missed payments. Accurate prebankruptcy late payments may remain. Reaffirmed debts, surviving obligations, and secured accounts can require different treatment.

Dispute specific errors with both the credit bureau and the company supplying the information. Include relevant supporting documents and keep copies of your submissions and responses. Correcting an error improves accuracy, but it does not guarantee a particular score increase.

Also review any new credit offers carefully. A “fresh start” label or preapproval does not establish that the terms are affordable.

## Months three to six: consider one manageable account

You do not need several new accounts to get started. If your budget supports it, one suitable product may help establish positive payment history.

### A secured credit card

A secured card generally requires a deposit that serves as collateral. It is still a credit card: purchases create a balance you must repay. The deposit ordinarily does not make your monthly payments for you.

Compare:

- Annual, monthly, application, and other fees.
- Interest rates and the availability of a purchase grace period.
- Which credit bureaus receive payment information.
- Deposit requirements and refund terms.
- Whether upgrading to an unsecured account is possible.

Graduation and a deposit refund are not guaranteed on a particular schedule.

### A credit-builder loan

These products generally hold loan proceeds in a restricted account while you make payments, with funds becoming available under the agreement’s terms.

Check interest, fees, access to funds, and reporting practices. A payment you cannot reliably afford can undermine the intended benefit.

You do not need both a secured card and a credit-builder loan. Choose based on cost and fit rather than the promise of faster results.

## Use the account without creating another debt problem

For a credit card, charge only amounts you already have money to pay. A small recurring expense can make activity easy to track.

Pay at least the required minimum by the due date, and aim to pay the full statement balance to avoid purchase interest when the account’s grace-period requirements are met.

Keep the reported balance low relative to the credit limit. A high balance can affect a score even if you pay it off shortly afterward, because reporting and scoring happen on different dates.

**You do not need to leave a balance unpaid or pay interest to build credit.**

Autopay and reminders can help, but monitor them. An automatic withdrawal from an underfunded account can cause fees or a failed payment. Paying only the minimum can prevent lateness while still allowing interest and debt to accumulate.

## Months six to twelve: maintain and reassess

Keep the account manageable and continue checking for errors. Avoid opening multiple accounts simply because more offers arrive.

Review whether the product is doing what you expected: reporting payments, charging the disclosed fees, and fitting your budget.

If you need a car, compare [financing](/library/renting-car-mortgage-after) based on total cost—not just approval or a monthly payment. Do not assume rates improve every six months or that refinancing will be available later.

A home purchase also has separate program requirements and waiting periods. This year’s payment history can help prepare you, but it does not guarantee mortgage eligibility on a particular date.

## Optional tools deserve scrutiny

### Authorized-user accounts

Being added to a trusted person’s account may help if the issuer reports it and the scoring model considers it. But high balances or negative activity can create problems.

The primary account holder remains responsible for authorized spending, so expectations and access should be clear. Do not treat the arrangement as risk-free for either person.

### Rent reporting

A service may add qualifying rent payments to one or more credit reports. Check its fees, which bureaus receive the information, and whether missed payments are reported.

Not every scoring model uses rental history. Paying for reporting therefore does not guarantee that a future lender’s score will improve.

## Set realistic expectations

A year of accurate reporting and consistent payments is useful progress, even if your score moves unevenly.

Different scores may change at different speeds. Approval, interest rates, and credit limits also depend on factors beyond the score.

Avoid measuring success only by access to larger loans. A smaller balance, fewer fees, reliable payments, and money available for emergencies can represent a stronger recovery than a higher limit.

## The bottom line

The first year is about establishing habits and obligations you can maintain.

Correct errors, protect your budget, and consider one reasonably priced credit-building account if it fits. Keep balances manageable and payments on time without borrowing more than you need.

The goal is credit that supports your financial life—not a new payment burden created to chase a score.
