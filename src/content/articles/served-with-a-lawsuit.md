## What the lawsuit means

A creditor or debt buyer is asking a court to establish that you owe a legally enforceable debt and enter a judgment.

For ordinary credit card or medical debt, a judgment generally gives the creditor access to collection tools it did not previously have, potentially including [wage garnishment](/library/can-bankruptcy-stop-wage-garnishment), [bank levies](/library/bank-account-frozen-levy), or liens. State law, exemptions, and additional procedures determine which tools are available.

Being sued does not mean the plaintiff has already proved its case. It also does not mean the debt is invalid simply because a debt buyer purchased it.

## First, identify the deadline

Find the court name, case number, plaintiff, and instructions for responding. Note when and how you received the papers.

Depending on the court, you may need to file a written answer, appear on a specified date, or do both. A phone call to the collection attorney generally does not substitute for the required court response.

Do not assume settlement discussions, a request for documents, or plans to file bankruptcy extend the deadline. Any extension needs to be effective under the court’s rules.

If you question whether the papers are genuine, verify the case through the court using independently obtained contact information. Do not ignore them while checking.

## What happens if you do nothing?

The plaintiff may request a default judgment if you fail to respond or appear as required.

Default procedures vary. A judgment is not necessarily automatic, and the plaintiff may still need to provide supporting information. But failing to participate can mean losing the opportunity to challenge the debt, amount, or legal basis before judgment.

The court may award the claimed balance and legally authorized interest, costs, or attorney fees. It does not follow that every amount requested is automatically recoverable.

## Your realistic options

### Respond and evaluate the claim

A proper response preserves your opportunity to contest the case. Review whether:

- You are the person legally responsible.
- The balance accounts for payments, credits, and any settlement.
- The plaintiff has the right to enforce the debt.
- The lawsuit was filed within the applicable limitation period.
- A prior bankruptcy discharge or another defense applies.

The plaintiff must satisfy the applicable evidentiary and legal requirements. Some claims have documentation problems; others are well supported. Requesting proof does not guarantee dismissal, and filing an answer is only the beginning of defending the case.

If the debt is old, the statute of limitations deserves attention. The applicable period and how it is calculated vary. In some states, a payment or acknowledgment can restart the period, so understand the consequences before making either.

### Negotiate a resolution

Settlement or a payment arrangement may be possible, but neither is guaranteed.

Get the terms in writing before paying. The agreement should explain the amount, payment schedule, what happens to the lawsuit, and whether any remaining balance is released.

Pay particular attention to consent judgments or provisions allowing judgment after a missed installment. A manageable-looking payment plan can carry significant enforcement consequences.

Keep meeting court deadlines unless the case has been resolved or an effective extension or stay applies.

### Evaluate bankruptcy in the context of all your debts

Bankruptcy may make sense when the lawsuit is part of a financial problem you cannot realistically resolve through repayment.

Filing generally stops an ordinary prefiling collection lawsuit when the [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay) applies. Eligible credit card, medical, and personal-loan debts may then be [discharged](/library/which-debts-does-bankruptcy-erase) or addressed through Chapter 13.

But filing is different from intending to file. If a deadline is days away, see [How fast can I file if something is days away?](/library/how-fast-can-i-file) Eligibility, property risks, prior cases, and any discharge exceptions must be considered. Bankruptcy should address the overall situation rather than simply postpone a court date.

## If a judgment has already been entered

You may still have options.

A court can sometimes set aside a default judgment based on improper service or another recognized ground. Grounds, procedures, and deadlines vary; acting quickly matters, but speed alone does not establish a right to relief.

Bankruptcy can often discharge personal liability on an ordinary collection judgment. A judgment generally retains the character of the underlying debt, so one based on fraud, support, or another exception may receive different treatment.

A judgment lien can survive unless it is properly avoided or otherwise resolved. Filing also does not automatically return money already collected through garnishment or levy.

## Keep the paperwork together

Save the summons and complaint, service information, account statements, collection letters, payment records, and any settlement or prior bankruptcy documents.

Track new notices and hearing dates. If garnishment or a bank levy begins, retain those documents too; they may have separate response or exemption deadlines.

A lawsuit requires attention even if your income or property is [protected from collection](/library/judgment-proof-doing-nothing). Those protections do not necessarily prevent a judgment from being entered.

## The bottom line

A debt collection lawsuit creates a deadline, not an automatic defeat.

Identify what the court requires, preserve your defenses, and evaluate whether contesting, settling, or filing bankruptcy fits your broader finances. Do not let negotiations or a possible future filing leave the lawsuit unanswered.

If judgment has already been entered, address both the underlying debt and any liens or collection actions it created.
