## If you have decided to file

Once bankruptcy is the chosen path, it may make sense to stop paying ordinary unsecured credit cards that are expected to be discharged. That money may be needed for housing, food, utilities, insurance, and the costs of filing.

But **deciding to file does not end your legal obligation to pay or create bankruptcy protection**. Until the case is filed, creditors generally retain their collection rights. Hiring an attorney alone does not trigger the [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay).

The decision should account for the expected filing date, whether particular debts may survive, and any collection action already underway.

Stopping credit card payments does not mean stopping every payment. Ongoing support, essential living expenses, taxes, and loans secured by property you intend to keep require separate attention.

## If you have not decided

Missing payments can lead to late fees, interest, account closure, collection activity, and credit damage. Creditors may sue, and a judgment can lead to [garnishment](/library/can-bankruptcy-stop-wage-garnishment), a [bank levy](/library/bank-account-frozen-levy), or a lien, depending on applicable law.

There is no dependable grace period during which every creditor will wait.

Bankruptcy can often address the underlying debt and stop collection when the stay applies, but it does not automatically recover money already collected or remove every judgment lien.

If you receive court papers, do not ignore the response deadline because you expect to file bankruptcy later.

The useful sequence is to understand your options, establish a realistic plan, and then decide which payments to change.

## What to avoid before filing

### Running up new charges

Do not use an expected bankruptcy discharge as a reason to take on debt you do not intend to repay.

Certain luxury purchases within 90 days before filing and qualifying cash advances within 70 days can trigger a presumption of nondischargeability when statutory thresholds are met. Those thresholds adjust periodically.

Necessary household purchases are excluded from the statutory definition of luxury goods, but that does not make every charge automatically safe. Fraud can be alleged outside those periods or below the thresholds; inability to pay alone does not establish fraud.

Waiting a particular number of days does not erase the circumstances in which a debt was incurred.

### Favoring family or particular creditors

Large or unusual repayments before bankruptcy can create preference issues.

The usual preference period is 90 days before filing. For qualifying insider transactions, including certain [repayments to relatives](/library/loans-from-family-and-friends), it can extend to one year.

Recovery is not automatic. The trustee must establish the legal requirements, and defenses may apply. There is no simple rule that every payment above $600 must be returned, or that every ordinary monthly payment is protected.

Disclose recent repayments accurately rather than trying to decide on your own which ones matter.

### Using protected assets to pay dischargeable debt

Qualifying [retirement savings](/library/retirement-accounts-in-bankruptcy) may receive substantial bankruptcy protection. Withdrawing them to pay credit cards can reduce that protection and create taxes or an additional early-distribution tax.

Likewise, using home equity to pay unsecured cards can replace dischargeable unsecured debt with a loan secured by your house.

Before moving money or borrowing, understand both the debt being paid and the new consequences being created.

### Transferring or hiding property

Do not give away property, remove your name from titles, or sell assets below their value to keep them out of bankruptcy.

Certain transfers can be reversed, and fraudulent transfers or concealment can jeopardize your discharge. A legitimate sale is not automatically prohibited, but its terms, proceeds, and disclosure matter.

Accurate paperwork is essential even when you believe the property would have been protected anyway.

## Practical issues to check

Before changing payments, review:

- **Automatic payments.** Know which withdrawals are scheduled. Canceling an authorization does not cancel the debt.
- **Recurring charges.** Move essential services off a card that may be closed, and cancel subscriptions you no longer need.
- **Shared accounts.** Your filing generally does not release a co-borrower from liability. An authorized user may have a different legal status.
- **Accounts at the same institution.** If you owe money where you bank, account freezes, setoff rights, or credit-union agreements may require separate analysis.
- **Secured cards and unusual agreements.** Not every account labeled a credit card is an ordinary unsecured obligation.

You generally must disclose all debts, including accounts you hoped to keep. Paying one card down does not guarantee the issuer will leave it open after bankruptcy.

## If you are not going to file

Stopping payments does not itself resolve the debt. It may be part of a settlement strategy or the unavoidable result of an insufficient budget, but collection risks remain.

Depending on your circumstances, [alternatives](/library/alternatives) may include a creditor hardship program, nonprofit credit counseling, or a debt-management plan. [Settlement](/library/debt-settlement-how-it-works) can involve fees, lawsuits while money accumulates, and possible tax consequences.

Having income or assets [protected from collection](/library/judgment-proof-doing-nothing) can affect your options, but those protections should be evaluated rather than assumed.

## The bottom line

If bankruptcy is the selected path, paying credit cards that are expected to be discharged may not be the best use of limited funds. But the obligation and collection risks continue until bankruptcy protection or another legal resolution applies.

Make the payment decision as part of a realistic filing plan. Protect essential expenses, avoid new borrowing and selective repayments that could complicate the case, and address lawsuits or collection deadlines promptly.
