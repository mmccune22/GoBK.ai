## Why student loans are different

Most student loans receive special treatment under bankruptcy law. For loans covered by the exception, an ordinary discharge order generally does not eliminate the debt.

You usually must file an **adversary proceeding**, a separate lawsuit within your bankruptcy case, and establish that repayment would impose an **undue hardship** on you and your dependents.

That requirement applies in both Chapter 7 and Chapter 13. Simply listing the loans, completing a repayment plan, or showing that the payments are difficult does not ordinarily discharge them.

But the rule is not “student loans can never be discharged.” The law provides a path to relief, and some education-related debts do not require an undue-hardship showing at all.

## What changed in 2022

In November 2022, the Department of Justice and Department of Education introduced a [standardized process](https://www.justice.gov/ust/student-loan-guidance) for evaluating requests to discharge federal student loans held by the Department of Education.

The process uses an attestation form describing your income, expenses, education, employment, and repayment history. Supporting records may be needed.

Government attorneys evaluate the information and may agree to relevant facts and recommend a full or partial discharge when appropriate. **The bankruptcy judge makes the final decision.** Submitting the form alone does not discharge a loan or replace the adversary proceeding.

As of this article’s update, DOJ continues to publish the guidance and an attestation form updated in May 2025. This is agency guidance rather than a change to the Bankruptcy Code. Procedures and policies can change, so their current application should be confirmed when a case is evaluated.

## What undue hardship means

The legal test depends on the controlling court decisions where your case is filed.

Many courts use a three-part framework examining whether:

- Repayment would prevent you from maintaining a minimal standard of living.
- Your inability to pay is likely to persist for a significant part of the repayment period.
- You have made good-faith efforts to repay.

Other courts examine the totality of your circumstances. The federal guidance addresses similar questions about present ability to pay, future prospects, and good faith, but it does not replace the court’s legal standard.

## Circumstances that may support relief

Factors worth examining include:

- A disability, chronic illness, or injury that limits earning ability.
- Retirement age and limited future income.
- A lengthy unemployment history.
- Leaving school without completing the degree.
- A long repayment history without a realistic path to paying the debt.
- Necessary household expenses that leave no ability to make payments.

No single factor guarantees discharge. You also do not have to fit every example to have a potentially viable case.

Good faith is broader than whether you made substantial payments. Under the federal guidance, efforts to earn income, manage expenses, and communicate about repayment options can matter. Past nonpayment does not automatically disqualify you, nor does failing to enroll in an income-driven plan when there is a reasonable explanation.

Likewise, being employed or having graduated recently does not automatically defeat a request. The question is how the applicable standard fits your actual circumstances.

## How to interpret success-rate claims

Government reports have shown substantial relief among borrowers whose cases reached a decision under the standardized process.

Those results are encouraging, but they do not establish the odds for every borrower considering bankruptcy. Decided cases involve people who pursued an adversary proceeding, often after their circumstances were evaluated. A success rate for that group is not a promise that the same percentage of all student-loan borrowers would qualify.

The useful question is whether your evidence supports relief under the law applicable to your case.

## Private loans require a separate analysis

The DOJ process does not govern every federal loan or private student loan. Confirm who owns the debt; the company servicing it may not be the owner.

For private education debts, start by determining whether the obligation falls within any part of the statutory student-loan exception.

Some education-related loans fall outside it. Examples that may warrant closer review include certain loans exceeding the school’s cost of attendance, loans for ineligible institutions or programs, and some bar-study or residency loans.

These are screening questions, not automatic exemptions from the rule. The loan documents, educational expenses, program eligibility, and applicable law determine the answer.

If the debt falls outside the exception, it may be discharged like ordinary unsecured debt without proving undue hardship. If it falls within the exception, an undue-hardship proceeding generally remains necessary.

## If the student loans survive

Bankruptcy may still provide meaningful relief by [eliminating other eligible debts](/library/which-debts-does-bankruptcy-erase) and making room in your budget.

[Chapter 13](/library/how-chapter-13-works) can address student loans within a repayment plan, but completing the plan does not ordinarily eliminate the remaining protected loan balance. Interest may continue accruing, potentially leaving a substantial amount due afterward.

The plan should therefore account for what happens to the student loans during the case and after it ends.

## The bottom line

Student loans are harder to discharge than ordinary unsecured debts, but relief is possible.

The first questions are what kind of debt you have, who owns it, and whether the student-loan exception applies. If it does, the next question is whether your circumstances support an undue-hardship discharge.

Do not assume the loans are untouchable—and do not assume a general bankruptcy discharge will erase them. The necessary legal process and evidence matter.
