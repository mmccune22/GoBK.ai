## The idea

Bankruptcy is designed to provide debt relief without necessarily leaving you with nothing.

When you file, your ownership interests in most property become part of a legal “bankruptcy estate.” Some property, including interests in certain qualifying retirement plans, may be excluded altogether.

Exemptions let you protect property or a specified amount of its value from being used to pay creditors. You must disclose your property and properly claim the available exemptions; protection is not simply a matter of leaving something off your paperwork.

In Chapter 7, the [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) reviews whether anything can be sold to produce money for creditors after accounting for liens, exemptions, and the costs of administering the property.

Most individual Chapter 7 cases are **no-asset cases**, meaning there are no assets available for a distribution to unsecured creditors. That does not mean the person owns nothing. It also does not necessarily mean every item is exempt: some property has no available equity or too little value to justify a sale.

## What is typically protected?

The categories and limits vary, but exemptions commonly cover:

- **Home equity**, through a homestead exemption.
- **Vehicle equity**, up to the applicable limit.
- **Household belongings**, such as furniture, appliances, and clothing.
- **Tools and equipment used for work**, subject to limits and conditions.
- **Qualifying retirement savings.**
- **Certain benefits, insurance interests, or wages.**
- **Other property covered by a wildcard exemption**, where available.

An exemption may protect an entire category of property or only a limited dollar amount. Some protections have additional conditions, such as how the property is used or where particular funds came from.

### Equity matters more than the purchase price

Suppose your car is worth $15,000 and the loan payoff is $12,000. Your equity is approximately $3,000. The applicable exemptions may protect that equity even though the car’s total value is much higher.

If you own the same car outright, you have approximately $15,000 in equity, so you need more protection to keep its entire value out of reach.

Property values must be realistic and supportable. Used household belongings generally are worth less than their original purchase price, but “garage-sale value” is not a universal rule for every asset or bankruptcy purpose.

## Which exemption laws apply?

Bankruptcy is federal law, but state law plays a major role in protecting property.

Some states allow eligible filers to choose between the federal bankruptcy exemptions and the applicable state exemption system. Others generally require use of state exemptions, together with any additional protections available under federal law.

Where a choice exists, you generally choose a system rather than selecting the most generous item from each list.

Recent moves can complicate the answer. Federal residency rules generally look first at where you were domiciled during the 730 days before filing. If you did not live in one state throughout that period, an earlier period becomes relevant. Additional rules can affect eligibility and homestead protection.

Your current address alone may therefore give the wrong answer.

The [state-by-state homestead and vehicle guide](/library/state-homestead-vehicle-exemptions) provides a starting point. The amount available in your case also depends on the filing date, ownership, eligibility requirements, and other applicable limits.

## What happens to property that is not fully exempt?

### In Chapter 7

If property has meaningful unprotected value, the trustee may sell it. Valid liens, allowable exemptions, sale costs, and other expenses affect whether a sale would benefit creditors.

Sometimes an agreement allows the filer or another person to pay the estate for its interest instead of having the property sold. That requires an acceptable arrangement and any necessary court approval; it is not a guaranteed right.

If property is burdensome or has inconsequential value to the estate, the trustee may abandon it, meaning the estate gives up its interest.

### In Chapter 13

You can often keep nonexempt property by funding a [repayment plan](/library/how-chapter-13-works) that meets the legal requirements.

Unsecured creditors generally must receive at least what they would receive in Chapter 7. That is not necessarily the property’s full market value: liens, exemptions, and the costs of a hypothetical Chapter 7 sale affect the comparison.

Your income and other required payments matter too. Chapter 13 provides a way to protect property, but the resulting plan must be affordable and legally sufficient.

## Exemptions do not erase liens

Protecting equity from the trustee is different from dealing with a creditor that has a lien on the property.

A homestead exemption generally does not eliminate your mortgage. A vehicle exemption generally does not eliminate your car lender’s lien. Keeping financed property still requires addressing the secured debt.

Some judgment liens can be avoided when they impair an exemption, but that requires meeting specific legal requirements and taking the necessary steps. Tax liens and certain other obligations have special rules.

Exempt property is therefore protected for particular purposes—not automatically shielded from every creditor or every kind of collection.

## The bottom line

Exemptions are a major reason people can obtain bankruptcy relief while keeping the property they need for daily life.

The answer depends on three things: what you own, how much equity it has, and which protections apply. Loans and other liens require separate attention.

Before assuming you will lose something—or that everything is protected—work through those questions.
