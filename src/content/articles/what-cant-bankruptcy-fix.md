## Debts it can’t end

Bankruptcy can erase many debts, but some generally survive: child support and alimony, certain taxes, most student loans, criminal fines and restitution, and debts for injuries or deaths caused by driving under the influence. Debts obtained through fraud can also survive if the creditor successfully challenges their discharge.

Discharging student loans generally requires a separate court proceeding called an adversary proceeding, which is basically a lawsuit in the bankruptcy court. In that proceeding, you must show that repaying the loans would impose an undue hardship. For more detail, see [Which debts does bankruptcy erase, and which survive?](/library/which-debts-does-bankruptcy-erase)

If your main debt survives bankruptcy, filing may still help by eliminating other debts and freeing up money to address what remains.

## Loan terms it generally can’t change

Bankruptcy can eliminate your personal responsibility for many debts, but it generally does not remove a lender’s lien—the right to take back property securing a debt if you don’t pay. A mortgage or car loan generally remains secured by the property, even after your personal liability is discharged.

If you want to keep your home or car, you generally need to keep paying for it. Chapter 7 generally cannot change the loan’s balance or interest rate. Chapter 13 may help you catch up on missed mortgage payments or change the terms of certain car loans, but it generally cannot change the balance or interest rate on a mortgage secured only by your primary residence. Some liens can also be reduced or removed, depending on the circumstances.

Eliminating other debts may free up enough money to make your home or car payment manageable. The question is whether keeping the property fits your finances after bankruptcy.

## People it can’t protect

Your discharge generally does not eliminate another person’s responsibility for a shared debt. A cosigner, a spouse who borrowed with you but did not file, or a parent on your car loan may still owe the money.

Debt is often a family problem. A married person can file bankruptcy individually, but if both spouses are responsible for a debt, one spouse’s filing generally does not eliminate the other spouse’s obligation. For both spouses to discharge their personal responsibility for a shared debt, both generally need to file. They can usually do this together in a joint bankruptcy case.

Chapter 13 provides a temporary co-debtor stay that can protect certain cosigners, including a nonfiling spouse, on consumer debts. That protection has limits, and a creditor may ask the court for permission to collect. Chapter 7 does not provide the same co-debtor protection.

If your goal is to address both spouses’ debts or protect someone who shares your debt, bring it up at the consultation. It can change the strategy.

## The past it can’t undo

Timing matters. A completed foreclosure sale may mean the opportunity to save the home has passed. Some recently garnished wages may be recoverable, depending on the circumstances, but recovered funds typically go to the bankruptcy estate rather than directly to you.

Giving away property or transferring it to relatives before filing can create serious problems. Even transfers made with good intentions must be disclosed when required and could be reversed.

Get informed before withdrawing retirement funds, transferring property, or using savings to pay debts. Understanding your options first can help you preserve resources and avoid decisions that are difficult to undo.

## The life it can’t build

Bankruptcy can eliminate debt, but it cannot solve every financial problem. It does not directly raise your income or lower your ongoing living expenses. If your expenses continue to exceed your income after bankruptcy, financial pressure will remain.

That does not mean bankruptcy cannot help. It means the plan should account for both the debts you can eliminate and the expenses you will still have. Expenses may need to be reduced substantially. Keeping every financed asset may not be realistic.

In some cases, surrendering a home, car, or other property securing a loan may be part of the solution. Bankruptcy can generally discharge your personal responsibility for the remaining loan debt, allowing you to move forward without that payment.

## The bottom line

Bankruptcy can provide meaningful relief, even when it cannot fix every part of your financial situation. It can eliminate many debts and create room to address the obligations and expenses that remain.

The goal is to understand what bankruptcy can accomplish for you, what it will leave unresolved, and what changes may be needed to make the fresh start last.
