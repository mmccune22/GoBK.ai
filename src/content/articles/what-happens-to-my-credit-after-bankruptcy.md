## How long it stays on your report

A voluntary Chapter 7 bankruptcy generally can remain on your credit report for up to ten years from filing. Credit bureaus typically remove Chapter 13 after seven years, although the general federal bankruptcy-reporting limit is ten years.

The bankruptcy entry and the individual accounts have separate reporting timelines. Discharge does not automatically delete accurate late payments or other prefiling account history.

The reporting period is not a waiting period during which rebuilding is impossible. See [How long does bankruptcy stay on my credit report?](/library/when-does-bankruptcy-fall-off-credit-report) for the details.

## What happens to your score?

Bankruptcy is a serious negative factor in credit scoring. Someone with strong credit before filing may experience a different change from someone whose report already contains extensive missed payments, collections, and high balances.

There is no dependable promise that your score will rise by a certain amount—or recover within twelve or twenty-four months. Results depend on the scoring model, the rest of your report, and what happens afterward.

Your credit score also is not the same as your financial health. Eliminating eligible debts may make your budget much more manageable even while the bankruptcy remains a negative scoring factor. Income does not directly enter a FICO score, although lenders can consider income and repayment capacity separately.

The goal is a stable financial position that can support positive credit history over time—not simply a higher number as quickly as possible.

## What should discharged accounts show?

For an ordinary unsecured account whose personal liability was discharged, reporting generally should reflect that you no longer owe a collectible balance. It should not continue presenting the discharged obligation as currently past due.

But not every account included in bankruptcy must be reported identically. [Reaffirmed debts](/library/reaffirmation-retain-redeem), [surviving obligations](/library/which-debts-does-bankruptcy-erase), mortgages, and accounts during an ongoing Chapter 13 case can require different analysis.

Review the account’s balance, status, payment history, and bankruptcy notation together. A historical late payment from before filing is different from a new late payment improperly attributed to a discharged balance.

Obtain reports from all three nationwide credit bureaus and dispute specific inaccuracies with the bureau and the company furnishing the information. Keep supporting records and copies of your disputes. You can access reports through [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/).

## Getting a car loan

Car financing may be available after bankruptcy, but approval and affordability are separate questions.

Compare the interest rate, fees, loan length, total repayment cost, and vehicle price. A low monthly payment can conceal an expensive long-term loan.

Do not buy or finance a car solely to improve your score. Taking on a payment that strains your budget can undermine the fresh start.

If your bankruptcy is still pending, new borrowing may require approval. Check the applicable requirements before signing.

## Getting a mortgage

Bankruptcy does not permanently prevent homeownership. Mortgage programs have different eligibility rules and waiting periods.

The relevant clock may run from discharge, dismissal, or a period of successful Chapter 13 payments—not simply the bankruptcy filing date. A separate foreclosure or other credit event may also affect eligibility.

Meeting a waiting period does not guarantee approval. Income, credit history, down payment, debt obligations, and lender requirements still matter.

See [Renting, car loans, and mortgages after bankruptcy](/library/renting-car-mortgage-after) for the program-specific discussion.

## Renting after bankruptcy

Landlords use different screening standards. Some consider bankruptcy alongside income, rental history, references, and current obligations; others have more restrictive policies.

Do not assume every landlord will reject an application or overlook the filing. Ask about screening criteria before paying an application fee when possible.

A short, factual explanation of the circumstances and your current ability to pay may help. Any additional deposit or screening requirement remains subject to applicable law.

## Building positive history

Start with a budget that allows essential bills to be paid reliably. Then consider whether a modest credit product fits that budget.

A reasonably priced secured credit card can help establish payment history if it reports to the credit bureaus. Keep reported balances low and pay the statement balance in full by the due date to avoid interest.

A credit-builder loan may be another option, but compare its costs and terms. You do not need both products, multiple new accounts, or interest-bearing balances simply to rebuild.

Be cautious about expensive “fresh start” offers and promises to remove accurate bankruptcy information. Correcting errors and building new history are different from deleting an accurate record.

See [Rebuilding credit after bankruptcy: a realistic first year](/library/rebuilding-credit-first-year) for a more detailed approach.

## Chapter 13 and credit

Chapter 13 is not simply a three-to-five-year credit freeze. Your financial circumstances and credit information can change while the plan is pending.

However, [plan obligations](/library/how-chapter-13-works) come first, and new borrowing may require court or [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) approval under applicable procedures. An approval is not guaranteed because the purchase seems reasonable.

Do not assume trustee payments automatically produce the same credit-reporting history as payments on a conventional loan. Likewise, treatment of mortgage and car payments can vary.

The immediate objective is a feasible plan and successful completion. New credit should support that objective rather than compete with it.

## The bottom line

Bankruptcy can damage credit while improving the underlying finances that make rebuilding possible. Both can be true at the same time.

Accurate reporting, manageable obligations, and consistent payments matter more than chasing a promised recovery date. You may regain access to credit before the bankruptcy disappears, but affordable terms and a sustainable budget are the measures that count.
