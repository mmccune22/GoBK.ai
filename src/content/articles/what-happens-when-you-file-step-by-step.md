## Before filing: where most of the work happens

**Reviewing your situation.** The first step is understanding your income, debts, property, and goals. Are you trying to stop a garnishment, address credit cards, or save a home? The answers help determine whether to file, which chapter fits, and when filing makes sense. Sometimes waiting or choosing an alternative is the better option. Filing requires detailed information about your income, debts, property, and recent financial history.

**Completing credit counseling.** Most individual filers must complete [credit counseling](/library/credit-counseling-and-debtor-education) from an approved provider within the 180 days before filing. The session generally takes about an hour and is commonly available online or by phone. A separate debtor education course comes after filing.

**Preparing and reviewing the paperwork.** The petition, schedules, and related forms describe your debts, property, income, expenses, and financial history. If you have an attorney, they prepare these documents using the information you provide.

Read everything carefully before signing. These are sworn disclosures, signed under penalty of perjury. Include debts and property even if you think they are protected, unimportant, or already known to your attorney. If something is wrong or missing, speak up so it can be corrected.

## Filing day

Your paperwork is filed with the bankruptcy court, usually electronically. Filing creates your case and assigns a case number. Most people do not attend court or appear anywhere on filing day.

The [automatic stay](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay) generally takes effect immediately and stops most collection activity, including many collection lawsuits, garnishments, and creditor demands for payment. It has exceptions, and prior bankruptcy filings can limit whether it begins or how long it lasts.

The court sends notice to the creditors listed in your paperwork. If a creditor contacts you before receiving notice, provide your case number and, if you have one, your attorney’s contact information.

Filing does not end all your financial responsibilities. Depending on your case and what you intend to keep, you may need to continue mortgage payments, car payments, insurance, and other ongoing expenses.

In Chapter 13, plan payments generally begin within 30 days after filing the plan or the case, whichever is earlier. You do not wait for the court to approve the plan before starting payments.

## About a month in: the 341 meeting

The “meeting of creditors,” also called the [341 meeting](/library/what-is-a-341-meeting), usually takes place several weeks after filing. The [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) conducts it; a judge does not attend.

You answer questions under oath about your paperwork, property, and financial circumstances. Creditors may participate, but they often do not attend routine consumer cases.

In a straightforward case, the questioning may take only five to ten minutes. More complicated cases can take longer, and the trustee may continue the meeting to another date if information is missing or additional questions need answers.

Before the meeting, review your paperwork, provide the requested documents, and follow the instructions for identification and attendance. Many meetings are held remotely, so check the notice for how to participate.

The meeting is usually a short fact-finding step, rather than a courtroom trial.

## The quiet stretch—and what you still need to do

After the meeting, a straightforward Chapter 7 may become relatively quiet. But you still have responsibilities.

Complete the approved [debtor education course](/library/credit-counseling-and-debtor-education) promptly after filing and make sure completion is properly documented with the court. This course takes at least two hours and is separate from pre-filing credit counseling. Failing to complete it can prevent a discharge and result in the case closing without one.

Continue responding to requests from your attorney or trustee. If you discover an error, receive unexpected money or property, or experience a significant financial change, report it promptly so its effect on the case can be evaluated.

Deadlines also run during this period. Certain objections to discharge or to the dischargeability of particular debts generally must be filed within 60 days after the first date set for the creditors’ meeting. Other issues have different deadlines, and the trustee’s work does not necessarily end when that period expires.

In Chapter 13, this period is more active. You continue making payments, and the trustee or creditors may raise questions about the proposed plan. The plan may need changes before the court confirms it.

## The finish: discharge and case closure

### Chapter 7

In a straightforward Chapter 7, the court generally enters a discharge about 60 to 90 days after the first date set for the creditors’ meeting, assuming the requirements are met and no unresolved issue prevents it. That often puts the discharge roughly three to four months after filing.

The discharge removes your personal obligation to pay dischargeable debts and prohibits creditors from collecting those debts from you personally. Some debts survive, and liens generally remain unless separately addressed.

Keep the discharge order and a copy of your bankruptcy paperwork.

**Discharge and case closure are different events.** A simple case may close around the time of discharge. If the trustee is selling property, recovering funds, or distributing money to creditors, the case can remain open longer.

### Chapter 13

Chapter 13 usually continues for three to five years under a court-approved repayment plan. During that time, you make required payments, meet ongoing obligations, and address relevant changes in your finances.

After completing the plan and satisfying the remaining requirements, you generally receive a discharge. The trustee completes the final administration, and the court closes the case.

The [Chapter 13 section](/library/chapter-13) explains that longer process in detail.

## The bottom line

Most of the preparation happens before filing, but your responsibilities continue afterward. Accurate paperwork, timely responses, required courses, and ongoing payments all help the case move forward.

A straightforward Chapter 7 often involves one short meeting and a few months from filing to discharge. Chapter 13 requires a longer commitment. Either process can involve additional questions or complications, even when you have been honest and careful.

Knowing what happens next—and what you need to do at each stage—makes the process more manageable.
