## Modify the plan

If your income drops or a necessary expense increases, you may be able to change your plan rather than abandon the case.

A modification can sometimes lower payments, extend the repayment period, or change how particular debts are treated. But the revised plan must still meet legal requirements. Mortgage arrears, priority debts, and the minimum owed to unsecured creditors can limit how much the payment can drop.

A plan already running for five years generally cannot be extended further. Surrendering property after confirmation also does not automatically eliminate the remaining secured debt; the available treatment depends on the circumstances and applicable court decisions.

Modification requires court approval and updated financial information. Until a change is approved or the court orders otherwise, your existing payment obligations remain in effect. Contact your attorney when the problem develops rather than waiting for several payments to be missed.

## Convert to Chapter 7

If repayment is no longer realistic, converting the existing case to Chapter 7 may provide another route to debt relief. You generally have a right to convert, subject to Chapter 7 eligibility requirements. Conversion does not guarantee a discharge.

Two questions deserve particular attention:

**Can you receive a Chapter 7 discharge?** Prior cases matter. For example, if you received a Chapter 7 discharge, the [eight-year waiting period](/library/how-often-can-you-file) generally runs between the filing date of that earlier case and the original filing date of your current Chapter 13—not the date you request conversion. Other prior discharges can trigger different rules.

**What happens to your property?** Property protected through the Chapter 13 repayment plan may be exposed to sale under Chapter 7. Conversion can also change the treatment of a [car loan](/library/chapter-13-keep-car-cramdown) or other secured debt. A balance reduction proposed or approved in Chapter 13 does not necessarily carry over into Chapter 7.

Conversion may solve an unaffordable plan, but it can also end the mechanism you were using to [catch up on a mortgage](/library/chapter-13-stop-foreclosure-catch-up-mortgage) or retain nonexempt property.

## The hardship discharge

A hardship discharge is a limited option for someone who cannot complete a confirmed plan. The court must find that:

- The failure to complete payments resulted from circumstances for which you should not justly be held accountable.
- Each allowed unsecured claim has received at least the legally required Chapter 7 comparison amount.
- Modifying the plan is not practicable.

A serious, lasting illness or disability may support a request, but financial difficulty alone does not automatically qualify.

The discharge is narrower than the discharge available after completing a Chapter 13 plan. More categories of debt survive, and it does not erase a mortgage lien or make missed mortgage payments disappear.

## Dismiss, and sometimes file again

If your case began under Chapter 13, you generally have the right to request dismissal. Different rules apply if the case was previously converted from another chapter.

Dismissal ordinarily means the case ends without a discharge. Stay protection ends, and creditors can generally resume collection of unpaid obligations, subject to applicable law and credit for payments received. Interest and charges may leave balances different from what you expected.

A later filing may be possible, but dismissal and refiling require careful attention to timing:

- **A possible 180-day filing bar.** Voluntarily dismissing after a creditor requests relief from the automatic stay can trigger a 180-day restriction under § 109(g)(2). The sequence and circumstances matter.
- **Reduced [stay protection](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay).** If one prior case was pending and dismissed within the preceding year, stay protection in the new case may terminate after 30 days unless extended. With two or more such prior cases, the stay may not arise at all unless the court imposes it. Exceptions and strict deadlines apply.
- **Other court restrictions.** A dismissal order may impose additional limits on refiling.

A new case is not an automatic reset. Before dismissing, understand whether another filing will be available and whether it will protect the property you are trying to keep.

## When your budget stops working

A financial setback does not mean you did something wrong. But missed payments can lead to dismissal, and missed mortgage or car payments can put property at risk.

Tell your attorney what changed, when it happened, and whether the problem appears temporary or lasting. Updated income information and a realistic expense picture help determine which options remain workable.

Do not assume that a possible modification, conversion, or hardship discharge suspends your obligations. Those steps require action, and some involve court approval and deadlines.

## The bottom line

An unaffordable Chapter 13 plan does not always mean you have run out of options. Modification, conversion, a hardship discharge, or dismissal may provide a path forward.

Each option changes what happens to your debts, property, and bankruptcy protection. Addressing the problem promptly gives you more time to choose a workable response before missed payments or court deadlines narrow the choices.
