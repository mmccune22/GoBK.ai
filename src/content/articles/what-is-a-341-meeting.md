## When and where

The court sends a notice stating the date, time, and instructions for attending. Read it carefully, along with any additional instructions from the trustee.

Almost all 341 meetings administered through the [U.S. Trustee Program](https://www.justice.gov/ust/moc) are now held virtually using Zoom. Follow the instructions for your particular case rather than assuming you should appear at the courthouse or join by telephone.

The meeting generally occurs several weeks after filing. The precise timing depends on the chapter and scheduling.

A bankruptcy judge does not attend. The [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) conducts the meeting, and the judge is prohibited by law from participating.

## Who is there

The participants generally include:

- The trustee.
- You and your attorney, if you have one.
- Your spouse, if you filed a joint case.
- Any creditors or their representatives who choose to participate.

Trustees often schedule several cases in a block, so you may hear other meetings while waiting for yours. Allow more time than the expected length of your own questioning.

**Both spouses must attend when they file together.** One spouse cannot answer for both.

Creditors are allowed to ask relevant questions, but their attendance does not turn the meeting into a trial. The trustee directs the proceedings, and disputes requiring a judge’s decision are generally addressed separately.

## What happens

The trustee confirms your identity, places you under oath, and asks questions about your bankruptcy paperwork and financial circumstances. Identification documents may need to be submitted before the meeting as well as kept available during it.

Common questions include:

- Did you review your paperwork before signing it?
- Is the information accurate, and are any corrections needed?
- Have you listed all your property and debts?
- Have you transferred or given away property?
- Have you repaid family members or other creditors before filing?
- Do you have a right to receive money from a tax refund, inheritance, legal claim, or another source?

The trustee may also ask questions specific to your case. A homeowner may be asked about the property’s value. A business owner may need to explain business assets or income. In Chapter 13, questions may address the budget and proposed repayment plan.

Answer truthfully and clearly. If you do not understand a question, ask for clarification. If you do not know an answer, say so rather than guessing.

Questions about an asset or transaction do not automatically mean you did something wrong. Investigating relevant financial matters is part of the trustee’s job.

## How to prepare

**Review your paperwork.** Refresh your memory about what was filed. If you discover an error or omission, tell your attorney promptly—or address the correction yourself if you are representing yourself.

**Provide requested records in advance.** The trustee may require identification documents, proof of income, bank statements covering the filing date, tax returns, and other records. Follow the stated deadlines and use the secure submission method provided. Do not assume bringing documents to the meeting will satisfy an earlier deadline.

**Have identification available.** Follow the instructions for acceptable photo identification and proof of your Social Security number. A Social Security card is one option, but other documents may be acceptable.

**Keep your bankruptcy paperwork nearby.** You may need to refer to it when answering a question.

**Prepare for a remote meeting.** Test your connection, camera, and microphone beforehand. Join from a quiet, private location where you can pay attention and answer questions. Do not participate while driving.

If you need an interpreter, an accommodation, or help with the required technology, raise that issue before the meeting.

## What happens afterward

The trustee may conclude the meeting or continue it to another date. A continued meeting may allow time to obtain records, clarify an answer, or investigate an issue. It does not automatically mean wrongdoing.

Even after the meeting is concluded, the trustee may request more information. Respond by the stated deadline and continue meeting your other obligations in the case.

### Chapter 7

In a straightforward no-asset case, there may be little additional activity for you after the meeting. You still need to complete [debtor education](/library/credit-counseling-and-debtor-education), make sure completion is documented with the court, and respond to any follow-up requests.

Certain objections to discharge or to the dischargeability of particular debts generally must be filed within 60 days after the **first date set for the meeting**. Postponing or continuing the meeting does not automatically restart that period.

The discharge often follows a few months after filing, but completing the meeting does not guarantee that every requirement has been satisfied. If the trustee is administering assets, the case may remain open after discharge.

### Chapter 13

You continue making required plan payments while the court considers confirmation of your repayment plan. The trustee may request documents or changes to the plan before recommending approval.

The court decides whether to confirm the plan. Whether you must attend a confirmation hearing depends on the court’s procedures and the circumstances of your case. Follow the notices and instructions you receive.

## Why it is usually more manageable than it sounds

The name “meeting of creditors” can suggest a room full of people demanding payment. In a routine consumer case, the experience is usually much simpler: the trustee asks questions about financial information you have already disclosed.

It is still a required examination under oath, so preparation matters. Missing the meeting or failing to provide requested information can jeopardize your case. If an emergency prevents attendance, contact your attorney or the trustee promptly rather than simply missing it.

Accurate paperwork and timely cooperation help the meeting go smoothly. They do not eliminate every possible question, and an honest filer may still need to explain a transaction, provide records, or attend a continued meeting.

The goal is straightforward: understand the process, arrive prepared, and answer truthfully.
