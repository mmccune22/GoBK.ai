## Step one: compare your income with the median

The calculation generally starts with income received during the six full calendar months before the month you file. Certain income is excluded.

For example, a September filing generally uses income received from March through August.

The counted income is divided by six to get a monthly average, then multiplied by twelve. That annualized figure is compared with the median income for your state and household size.

If you are at or below the median, you generally do not need to complete the second, expense-based calculation. You have cleared this part of the Chapter 7 analysis.

The [U.S. Trustee Program](https://www.justice.gov/ust/means-testing) publishes the median income figures and updates them periodically. The figures applicable to your filing date must be used.

## Step two: subtract allowed expenses

If your income is above the median, a more detailed calculation subtracts deductions allowed under bankruptcy law.

Some expenses use IRS national or local standards, including allowances for food, clothing, housing, and transportation. These allowances can depend on household size and location. Other deductions use qualifying actual expenses or specific calculations for obligations such as taxes, insurance, childcare, support, and secured debts.

The result is your disposable income under the means test. This can differ from what you actually have left in your checking account each month. Not every expense you pay qualifies as a deduction.

The remaining amount is measured against legal thresholds and, in some cases, the amount of your unsecured debt. If it is low enough, no presumption of abuse arises. If it is too high, the law presumes that Chapter 7 relief would be inappropriate unless that presumption can be overcome.

“Presumption of abuse” is a legal term about whether Chapter 7 is appropriate—not an accusation that you have done something dishonest.

See [I’m above the median income. Can I still file Chapter 7?](/library/above-median-income-now-what) for more about deductions and special circumstances.

## What counts as income?

The calculation can include:

- Wages, salary, overtime, commissions, and bonuses.
- Net income from a business or rental property.
- Pension and other retirement income.
- Support payments received.
- Regular contributions from other people toward household expenses.

Social Security benefits are excluded. Certain qualifying military disability and survivor benefits are excluded as well. Other payments, including unemployment benefits, can raise questions about how the rules apply.

Someone else’s income does not automatically count simply because they live with you. Regular contributions they make toward your household expenses may count.

A spouse’s income has separate rules. If you are married and living together, your spouse’s income generally must be considered even if only you file. An adjustment may apply to income that is not used for your household’s expenses.

The means test uses its own definition of income. A payment’s tax treatment does not necessarily determine whether it counts.

## When does the means test not apply?

The Chapter 7 means-test presumption generally does not apply when your debts are primarily nonconsumer debts, such as qualifying business debts.

There are also exceptions for certain disabled veterans and temporary exclusions for qualifying reservists and National Guard members who served on active duty or performed homeland defense activities.

These exceptions have specific requirements. Owning a business or having served in the military does not automatically create an exemption. Other bankruptcy requirements still apply.

## Why can the filing month matter?

Because the calculation looks backward, it may not reflect your finances today.

If you recently lost a job or had your hours reduced, the average may still include months of higher earnings. As those months leave the calculation, the result can change. A bonus or other one-time payment can also affect the average.

Timing should account for both the calculation and any urgent problems, such as [foreclosure](/library/can-bankruptcy-stop-foreclosure), [repossession](/library/stop-repossession-get-car-back), or [garnishment](/library/can-bankruptcy-stop-wage-garnishment). Waiting for a different means-test result is not always the best choice.

See [I’m above the median income. Can I still file Chapter 7?](/library/above-median-income-now-what) for more about changed circumstances.

## Is passing the means test enough?

No. Passing generally means the means-test calculation does not create a presumption of abuse. A Chapter 7 case may still be challenged based on bad faith or the overall financial circumstances.

Other eligibility rules also matter, and property protection is a separate issue. Passing the means test does not guarantee that everything you own is protected.

If Chapter 7 is not available or does not fit your needs, Chapter 13 may provide another option. It uses related income calculations, but the required plan payment also depends on other factors.
