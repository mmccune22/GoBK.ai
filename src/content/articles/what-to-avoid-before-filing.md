Considering bankruptcy doesn't mean you have to stop living your life. Paying rent, groceries, utilities, insurance, medical care, and a car payment you intend to keep is generally fine.

What deserves advice first is a major or unusual financial change. Some moves that feel responsible can create problems in a later case.

## Giving away or retitling property

Transferring a house, car, or other property to a relative or friend, or selling it for less than it's worth, can be reversed by a trustee. Federal law reaches transfers within two years before filing, and state law can reach further back. Serious cases can put your discharge at risk.

Property you simply keep and disclose may already be protected by exemptions. Moving it can create a problem that didn't exist.

## Repaying relatives or friends

Repaying a family member or friend before other creditors can feel like the right thing to do. But payments to relatives and other insiders during the year before filing can sometimes be recovered by the trustee, from the person you paid. For most other creditors, the period is 90 days. See [Loans from family and friends](/library/loans-from-family-and-friends).

## Withdrawing retirement money

Retirement accounts are often well protected in bankruptcy. Money withdrawn from them may not be, and withdrawals can trigger taxes and penalties. See [What happens to my 401(k), IRA, or pension?](/library/retirement-accounts-in-bankruptcy)

## New debt and cash advances

Borrowing or charging with no realistic intent to repay can lead a creditor to challenge whether that debt can be discharged. Larger luxury purchases and cash advances shortly before filing can be presumed nondischargeable. Talk to an attorney before using credit once bankruptcy is on the table.

## Accounts, tax refunds, and other assets

Changes that seem minor can matter:

- Keeping money at a bank where you also owe a debt. See [What happens to my bank accounts when I file bankruptcy?](/library/bank-accounts-when-you-file)
- Spending or assigning an expected tax refund. See [Will I lose my tax refund?](/library/tax-refunds-and-bankruptcy)
- Adding or removing names on accounts or titles, or selling significant property.

None of these is automatically wrong. Timing and details matter, which is why advice first helps.

## Tell your attorney everything

Bankruptcy paperwork is signed under penalty of perjury, and trustees review records closely. Tell your attorney about past transfers, repayments, and withdrawals, including ones you've already made.

A past mistake is a reason to get advice promptly, not a reason to assume bankruptcy is off the table. Disclosed issues can often be addressed through timing or the case itself. Not every problem can be fixed, and honesty does not guarantee a particular result, but concealment can lead to denial of a discharge and other serious consequences.
