## The bankruptcy reporting clock

For an ordinary voluntary bankruptcy, the reporting period generally starts when you file—not when you receive a discharge.

The nationwide credit bureaus generally report:

- **Chapter 7:** up to ten years from filing.
- **Chapter 13:** seven years from filing under their reporting practices.

The seven-year Chapter 13 period is a bureau practice rather than a separate seven-year limit in the federal bankruptcy-reporting statute. Federal law generally permits reporting bankruptcy for ten years, with exceptions for certain reports and transactions.

A discharge or dismissal does not automatically remove an accurate bankruptcy entry. Its status should, however, be reported accurately.

## Individual accounts have separate clocks

The bankruptcy entry and the accounts included in the case do not necessarily disappear together.

Most negative account history generally has a seven-year reporting period. For collections and charge-offs, federal law ties the period to the delinquency that immediately preceded collection or charge-off, with a statutory calculation that includes 180 days.

Selling the account, changing collectors, or updating its status does not create a new original delinquency date. Bankruptcy also does not restart that account’s reporting clock.

For example, a credit card account that became delinquent well before bankruptcy may stop appearing before the bankruptcy entry does.

A collection account’s recent “updated” date is not automatically evidence of improper re-aging. The important question is whether the underlying dates and resulting reporting period are accurate.

## Discharge does not erase accurate history

Discharge generally eliminates personal liability for covered debts. It does not require deletion of every accurate late payment, collection entry, or charge-off that preceded the bankruptcy.

An ordinary unsecured account whose liability was discharged generally should not continue showing a balance you presently owe or new missed payments on that discharged obligation.

But an account’s treatment matters. [Reaffirmed debts](/library/reaffirmation-retain-redeem), [surviving obligations](/library/which-debts-does-bankruptcy-erase), secured accounts, and debts during an ongoing Chapter 13 plan can present different reporting questions. Do not assume every account listed in the case must immediately show the same status.

Accurate negative information and inaccurate reporting are different problems. You can dispute the latter without having a right to delete the former.

## Credit can improve before the entry disappears

You do not necessarily have to wait seven or ten years to rebuild credit or qualify for another loan.

But there is no guaranteed recovery date. Bankruptcy remains a significant negative factor while it appears, and different scoring models and lenders evaluate the same report differently.

On-time payments, manageable balances, and avoiding new delinquencies can support improvement. A secured card or credit-builder product may help when its costs and payments fit your budget. Taking on unnecessary debt is not required.

Loan eligibility also involves more than a score. Income, existing obligations, down payment, and program-specific waiting periods can matter.

See [Rebuilding credit after bankruptcy: a realistic first year](/library/rebuilding-credit-first-year) for practical steps.

## Check the reports and correct specific errors

Review reports from all three nationwide bureaus through [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/).

Look for:

- The correct bankruptcy chapter, filing date, and case status.
- Accurate treatment of discharged accounts.
- Incorrect balances or new past-due amounts.
- Duplicate entries that misrepresent what you owe.
- Incorrect delinquency dates or information remaining beyond the applicable period.

If you find an error, dispute it with the credit bureau and the company that supplied the information. Identify exactly what is wrong and include copies of supporting documents. Keep the originals and a record of the dispute and response.

A discharge order can support your dispute, but it may not answer every account-specific question by itself.

If a creditor is also demanding payment of discharged personal liability, that raises a separate discharge-enforcement issue. See [A discharged creditor is still trying to collect. What do I do?](/library/discharged-creditor-still-collecting)

## Credit repair cannot guarantee early removal

You do not need to pay a company to exercise your right to dispute inaccurate information.

Be cautious about promises to erase an accurate bankruptcy, create a new credit identity, or guarantee a particular score. An accurate entry generally can remain for the applicable reporting period even after your finances improve.

## The bottom line

The bankruptcy entry generally remains for seven years for Chapter 13 under bureau practices and up to ten years for Chapter 7. Individual accounts follow separate reporting rules.

Those periods describe how long information may appear—not how long rebuilding must take. Focus on accurate reporting, affordable obligations, and consistent payment history while the older information ages.
