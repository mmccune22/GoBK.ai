## Debts bankruptcy generally can eliminate

Many debts that lead people to consider bankruptcy are eligible for discharge, including:

- Credit cards and store cards.
- [Medical bills](/library/can-bankruptcy-erase-medical-debt).
- Personal loans and unsecured lines of credit.
- [Payday loans](/library/payday-loans-and-bankruptcy).
- Eligible deficiency balances after repossession or foreclosure.
- Old utility and phone bills.
- Collection accounts based on these obligations.

A collection account generally keeps the legal character of the original debt. Selling a medical bill to a collector does not ordinarily make it nondischargeable. Likewise, obtaining a judgment does not automatically prevent discharge, although a resulting lien requires separate attention.

### Chapter 7 and Chapter 13 handle repayment differently

In Chapter 7, eligible unsecured debts can be discharged without requiring you to repay a portion from future income. A trustee may distribute money from nonexempt assets if any are available.

In Chapter 13, your plan may pay some or all of those debts before the remaining eligible balance is discharged. The required payment depends on your income, property, and other plan requirements.

Discharge eliminates covered personal liability and prohibits collection of that liability. It does not necessarily mean the creditor received nothing during the case.

### What about income taxes on canceled debt?

Debt discharged in a bankruptcy case is generally excluded from federal taxable income. Tax reporting may still be required, and the exclusion can reduce certain tax attributes, such as loss carryovers or the tax basis of property.

A Form 1099-C does not, by itself, mean you owe income tax on a debt discharged in bankruptcy.

## Debts that commonly survive

Important categories include:

- **[Child support and alimony](/library/child-support-alimony-and-bankruptcy).** Qualifying domestic support obligations are not discharged.
- **[Certain taxes](/library/can-old-tax-debt-be-discharged).** Recent income taxes, trust-fund taxes, and taxes involving missing returns, fraud, or evasion can survive. Older income taxes require a detailed timing analysis.
- **[Most student loans](/library/student-loans-and-bankruptcy).** Loans covered by the student-loan exception generally require an undue-hardship determination in a separate bankruptcy lawsuit.
- **[Criminal fines and restitution](/library/court-fines-tickets-restitution).** These generally survive. Other government penalties can receive different treatment depending on the chapter.
- **Debts for death or personal injury caused by unlawful intoxicated operation of a vehicle.**
- **Other debts covered by a statutory exception.** The list is broader than these common examples.

A surviving debt does not necessarily make bankruptcy pointless. Eliminating other obligations may leave more room to pay support, taxes, or student loans.

## Debts a creditor may challenge

Some exceptions usually require the creditor to bring a timely lawsuit and establish the legal grounds for nondischargeability.

Examples include debts involving fraud, embezzlement, or misuse of money while acting in certain positions of trust. In Chapter 7, debts for willful and malicious injury can also fall into this category.

Difficulty paying a debt is not, by itself, fraud. But false representations, certain recent luxury purchases, and cash advances can raise separate issues.

For many of these challenges, the deadline is **60 days after the first date set for the meeting of creditors**, not 60 days after the [meeting](/library/what-is-a-341-meeting) actually occurs. The court can extend the deadline on a timely request, and exceptions can apply when a creditor lacked adequate notice.

Not every discharge exception requires a creditor to sue by this deadline.

## What if a debt was left off the paperwork?

List all debts and creditors, including disputed debts, [family loans](/library/loans-from-family-and-friends), and obligations you believe will survive.

An omitted debt is not automatically discharged or automatically excluded from discharge in every case. The result depends on the chapter, notice, whether creditors had an opportunity to participate, the nature of the debt, and applicable law.

Some omitted ordinary debts may still be discharged in a no-asset Chapter 7 case. Other omissions can leave a debt enforceable or preserve a creditor’s right to challenge it.

If you discover an omission, address it promptly rather than assuming the discharge resolved it.

## Secured debts require a separate analysis

A mortgage or car loan generally involves both:

- Your personal obligation to pay.
- A lien giving the creditor rights in specified property.

A discharge may eliminate personal liability while leaving the lien intact. The creditor may then be unable to collect from you personally but still have the right to foreclose or repossess if the secured obligation is not addressed.

Keeping financed property therefore requires more than knowing whether the debt is dischargeable. Depending on the chapter and circumstances, the available options may include maintaining payments, [reaffirmation, redemption](/library/reaffirmation-retain-redeem), or [restructuring through a plan](/library/chapter-13-keep-car-cramdown).

Some liens can be avoided or modified through specific bankruptcy procedures. Those results are not automatic.

See [Will I lose my house if I file bankruptcy?](/library/will-i-lose-my-house-if-i-file-bankruptcy) and [Can I keep my car if I file bankruptcy?](/library/can-i-keep-my-car-in-bankruptcy) for those details.

## Chapter 13 can discharge some additional debts

The discharge after completing a Chapter 13 plan is broader in certain respects than a Chapter 7 discharge.

For example, qualifying [divorce property-settlement obligations](/library/divorce-debts-and-bankruptcy) and some noncriminal government penalties may be dischargeable after plan completion even though they would survive Chapter 7.

That broader protection does not apply to every debt, and a Chapter 13 [hardship discharge](/library/what-if-i-cant-finish-chapter-13) has narrower coverage than a discharge earned by completing the plan. Support obligations, certain taxes, most student loans, and other exceptions still require attention.

## The bottom line

Bankruptcy can eliminate many common debts, but the answer depends on the obligation and the chapter—not simply the creditor’s name or the account’s label.

Identify which debts are eligible for discharge, which must be repaid or otherwise addressed, and which are secured by property you want to keep. A useful bankruptcy plan accounts for both the relief you can obtain and the obligations that will remain afterward.
