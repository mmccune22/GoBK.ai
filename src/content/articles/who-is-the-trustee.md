## The job

### In Chapter 7

The trustee reviews your financial affairs and determines whether there is value available for creditors. That includes examining your property, claimed exemptions, financial records, and certain transactions before filing.

Property is not automatically sold just because you own it. The trustee considers exemptions, loans or liens against the property, ownership interests, and the costs of selling it. There must be a meaningful benefit to the bankruptcy estate.

Most individual Chapter 7 cases are “no-asset” cases. That means there is no property or money for the trustee to distribute to unsecured creditors—not that the person filing owns nothing.

If value is available, the trustee may sell property or seek to recover money. Sometimes an agreement allows the filer to keep an asset in exchange for a payment, but that is not guaranteed. The outcome depends on the asset, available funds, and applicable legal requirements.

The trustee also examines creditor claims when appropriate and can challenge improper claims or raise concerns about a filer’s right to a discharge. Their role involves administering the estate according to the law, not simply looking for things to sell.

### In Chapter 13

The trustee reviews your proposed repayment plan, income, expenses, property, and other financial information. They evaluate whether the plan meets legal requirements and whether you appear able to make the proposed payments.

The trustee may object to the plan or request changes. An objection does not necessarily mean you did something wrong. It may concern calculations, supporting documents, property values, or a disagreement about what the law requires.

The judge decides whether to confirm the plan. The trustee then administers payments and distributions to creditors and monitors the case over time. Their work can also include reviewing proposed changes to the plan and addressing missed payments or other compliance issues.

## What they look for

The trustee compares your paperwork with supporting records and your answers at the [341 meeting](/library/what-is-a-341-meeting). Common areas of review include:

- **Property and its value.** What you own, what it is worth, what you owe against it, and whether an [exemption](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions) protects your interest.
- **Transfers before filing.** Property sold, given away, or transferred to someone else, including relatives.
- **Payments to creditors or family members.** Certain payments before filing may be recoverable, even if you were paying a legitimate debt.
- **Income and financial records.** Wages, business income, bank deposits, and information needed to understand your finances.
- **Rights to money or property.** Tax refunds, legal claims, inheritances, and other interests that may belong to the bankruptcy estate.

**An honest filer can still have property or transactions the trustee must investigate.** A payment to a family member may have seemed ordinary. A property value may be uncertain. A [tax refund](/library/tax-refunds-and-bankruptcy) may be partly attributable to the period before filing.

Questions about these matters do not automatically mean fraud, dismissal, or loss of your discharge. They do mean the trustee may need records, explanations, or further legal review.

Full disclosure does not guarantee that property is protected or a transaction is beyond recovery. It allows those issues to be addressed accurately.

## How to deal with them

Be truthful, respectful, and responsive. At the 341 meeting, listen carefully and answer the question asked. If you do not understand a question, ask for clarification. If you do not know an answer, say so rather than guessing.

Provide requested records by the stated deadline. These may include bank statements, tax returns, titles, proof of income, or documents explaining a transaction. Cooperation and providing financial records are part of your responsibilities in the case.

If a record is unavailable or a deadline cannot be met, address the problem promptly rather than ignoring the request. If you discover an error in your paperwork, report it so it can be corrected.

When you have an attorney, coordinate document requests and legal questions through them. Your attorney can explain the request, help resolve disagreements, and advise you about your rights and obligations. The trustee does not represent you or provide legal advice on your behalf.

The trustee is also not the judge. Some disputes require a court ruling rather than an agreement at the creditors’ meeting.

## The bottom line

The trustee administers your case, investigates relevant financial matters, and carries out duties to creditors and the bankruptcy system.

Your part is to disclose your finances accurately, answer questions truthfully, provide requested records, and meet your ongoing obligations. A straightforward Chapter 7 may involve only a short meeting and limited follow-up. Other cases require more work, even when the filer has been honest and careful.

A request for more information is not, by itself, a sign that something has gone wrong. Understanding the trustee’s role helps you respond appropriately and keep the case moving.
