## The questions that matter

Start with three questions:

- How much equity do you have?
- How much of that equity is protected?
- Can you afford the mortgage and other ongoing costs?

**Equity** is the property’s current value minus the balances of mortgages and other valid liens. If your house is worth $350,000 and the mortgage payoff is $310,000, you have approximately $40,000 in equity, assuming there are no other liens.

A **homestead exemption** protects qualifying equity in your residence. See [What are exemptions?](/library/what-are-bankruptcy-exemptions) The available protection depends on the exemption laws that apply to you, ownership, and other conditions. Some exemption systems offer substantial protection; others offer much less.

Protected equity generally is not available to pay ordinary unsecured creditors. But an exemption does not eliminate your mortgage or guarantee protection against every lien.

## In Chapter 7

A Chapter 7 [trustee](/library/who-is-the-trustee) reviews whether selling your interest in the house would produce money for creditors after accounting for valid liens, the allowable exemption, sale costs, and other administration expenses.

If there is no meaningful value available for the estate, the trustee generally will not sell the house. If substantial unprotected value remains, a sale may be possible. The allowed exemption generally protects your share of the sale proceeds up to the applicable amount—not necessarily your ability to remain in the house.

Sale costs matter, but there is no universal percentage you can safely subtract in every case. Property values, actual costs, and applicable legal standards need to be evaluated realistically.

### The mortgage is a separate issue

A Chapter 7 discharge generally eliminates your personal liability for an eligible mortgage debt. It ordinarily does not remove the lender’s lien.

That means the lender may still foreclose if the loan is not paid as required, even when the trustee has no interest in selling the house.

Chapter 7 does not provide a repayment plan to spread mortgage arrears over several years. If you are behind, you need another workable way to resolve the default. The lender may ask the bankruptcy court for permission to proceed with foreclosure while the case is pending.

## In Chapter 13

Chapter 13 can offer a way to keep a house that would be at risk in Chapter 7.

If you are behind on the mortgage, a qualifying plan can generally spread the missed payments over three to five years while you maintain ongoing payments. You must file before the applicable foreclosure cutoff, and [stay protection](/library/what-is-the-automatic-stay) must be available.

For a typical long-term mortgage secured only by your principal residence, the usual approach is to catch up while maintaining the existing loan—not force a lower principal balance or interest rate. Exceptions require separate analysis.

If your house has nonexempt equity, the plan generally must pay unsecured creditors at least what they would receive in a hypothetical Chapter 7 case. That comparison accounts for more than the house’s value alone, including liens, exemptions, and relevant sale costs.

The resulting payment must be feasible. Chapter 13 can protect equity, but it cannot guarantee an affordable plan regardless of how much unprotected value you own.

## When a house may be at risk

Common situations include:

- Substantial unprotected equity in a Chapter 7 case.
- Mortgage arrears that you cannot resolve.
- A Chapter 13 payment that is unaffordable when combined with ongoing housing costs.
- New missed mortgage payments during Chapter 13.
- Unpaid property taxes, association assessments, or other obligations secured by enforceable liens.

A second mortgage or home equity line also needs attention. In some Chapter 13 cases, a wholly unsecured junior mortgage may be eligible for lien removal, but that requires the proper legal process and satisfaction of applicable requirements. It does not happen simply because you file bankruptcy.

## Things that can change the answer

### Recent moves and purchases

Your current state of residence does not necessarily determine which exemptions apply. Recent moves can point to another state’s law, and federal rules can limit homestead protection for certain recently acquired interests or transfers. The analysis is more specific than simply asking how long you have lived in the house.

### Joint ownership

Owning the house with a spouse, relative, or another person does not automatically prevent a trustee’s sale. Your ownership interest, the co-owner’s rights, available exemptions, and the potential benefit to creditors all matter. See [What happens to property I own with someone else?](/library/jointly-owned-property)

### The full cost of keeping the house

Affordability includes more than principal and interest. Account for property taxes, insurance, association dues, maintenance, and any required Chapter 13 payment. Avoid counting taxes and insurance twice if they are already included in your mortgage escrow payment.

### Foreclosure timing

If a sale is approaching, do not assume filing at any point will save the house. Applicable law determines the cutoff, and [prior bankruptcy cases](/library/how-often-can-you-file) may affect the automatic stay. See [How Chapter 13 saves a house: curing mortgage arrears](/library/chapter-13-stop-foreclosure-catch-up-mortgage).

## The bottom line

You can often obtain bankruptcy relief while keeping your house, but the answer requires looking at both equity protection and the mortgage.

Chapter 7 may work when the equity is protected and the loan can be maintained. Chapter 13 may help when you need time to catch up or protect nonexempt equity, provided the required payments fit a realistic budget.

Use the [homestead table](/library/state-homestead-vehicle-exemptions) as a starting point. Before relying on a listed amount, confirm the current exemption rules and how they apply to your ownership and filing history.
